  \noindent The purpose of this work is to show how the study of analytic aspects of the leaves of holomorphic foliations shows the analogies between the foliations and holomorphic maps, as a side effect, this thesis \emph{should} provide its author the title of doctor in mathematics.

  This work is the result of the research conducted during the course of my doctoral studies, both as a doctoral student and PhD candidate later, the main idea behind that I've worked on and developed durring this period is; analytic varieties, foliations and holomorphic functions are the same type of objects but of different kind. By being the same type of object I mean that they share a core of proposition that are true for all of them if they are enunciated properly. In this sense one can think of holomorphic foliations and analytic subsets on certain complex manifolds as the geometric version of the classical theory of holomophic/meromorphic functions.

  Together with my thesis advisor Dr.Santiago Alberto Verjovsky Solá, we studied holomorphic foliations $\mathfrak{F}$ whose leaves satisfy certain geometric properties that ensure a certain regularity in the structure of the leaf space of the foliation. We refer to regularity in the sense that normally the leaf space is topologically complex (non-Hausdorff), making it necessary to restrict the type of dynamics on the leaves of the foliation to guarantee some topological regularity in the leaf space. An example of these properties is the case of foliations whose leaves have \emph{uniformly bounded} volumes in compact K\"ahler manifolds $(M,h)$.

  The historical basis for studying foliations with bounded volume has roots in the works by mathematicians such as Epstein, Edwards, Millet, Reeb, Sullivan, Haefliger, J.C. Alexander, and Dr. Verjovsky himself, among others; see \cite{EMS}, \cite{V-A}, \cite{E-V}, \cite{Epstein1}, \cite{Epstein2}. However, the research we have proposed so far is supported by the previous work of J.C. Alexander and Dr. Verjovsky \cite{A-V}, which in turn is based on works by Errett Bishop \cite{Bishop} on extensions and Hausdorff limits of sequences of analytic varieties and follows the spirit of the presentation of Bishop's results found in the book ``Volumes, Limits, and Extensions of Analytic Varieties'' by Gabriel Stolzenberg \cite{Stolzenberg}.

  More precisely, we have been investigating ways to extend the range of possible applications of these results to various areas of complex analysis and complex geometry, with an emphasis on the theory of holomorphic foliations. We have found connections between known theorems of complex analysis and complex geometry. One of the first findings of these connections is, for example, a novel proof of Chow's theorem \cite{Chow}.

  First, the basic propperties of analytic sets and holomorphic manifolds are stablished, then there is a discussion on their analytic propperties, then I introduce Kähler manifolds and some of the most important properties are discussed particularly metric propperties, integration and volumes of their submanifolds. This naturarly leads to the definitions of the so called ``\emph{Lelong numbers}'' and of ``\emph{currents}'' as integration operators on analytic sets, which serve as analogues of residues and differential forms.

  Then, these propperties are used in the contexts of holomphic foliations, to study foliations with all leaves compact in Kähler manifolds, subsequently the same is done but for so called ``Moishezon varieties''.

  \section{Complex Analytic Varieties and Manifolds}
  This chapter is for stablishing the basic definitions, notations and conventions that will be used throughout this thesis.

  The set of complex numbers denoted as $\co$ and naturally the complex vector space of dimension $n$ will be denoted as $\con$. Given an open set $U\subset\con$ the sheaf of holomorphic functions at $U$, $f:U\rightarrow\co$ will be denoted as $\mathcal{H}(U)$ and $\osheaf_{z}$ will represent the sheaf of holomorphic germs near a point $z$, i.e. $\osheaf_{z}$ is the set of equivalence classes of holomorphic functions that coincide in an open neighborhood of $z$. I will note that the rings $\osheaf_{z}$ are \emph{isomorphic} for all $z\in\con$, so I will \emph{mostly} talk about $\osheaf_{0}$ wich will be denoted simply as $\osheaf_{n}$ denoting the dimension of the variety/manifold.

  Similarly, the set of \emph{biholomorphic} funtions between two spaces $U$ and $V$ i.e. the set of biyective holomorphic mappings with holomorphic inverse between $U$ and $V$ will be denoted as $\mathrm{Bil}(U,V)$, if $U=V$, then it will be denoted as $\mathrm{Bil}(U).$

  The set of complex lines in a $d$ dimensional complex vector spaces, also known as the complex projective space, will be denoted as $\cp^{d-1}$. Complex and real manifolds will be denoted usually with the letters $L, N, M,\dots$. etc. The tanget space of a manifold wil be denoted as $TM$ and the space of smooth sections (vector fields) of $TM$ will be denoted as $\mathcal{T}M$.

  An almost complex structure of an even dimensional real manifold will be denoted as $J:TM\rightarrow TM$, with $J²=-Id_{TM}$. Moreover the complexification of a even dimentional real manifold will be denoted as $TM_{\co}:=TM\otimes\co$, and the complex conjugate splitting will be denoted as $TM_{\co}=TM^{1,0}\oplus TM^{0,1}$.
  Similarly the space of $(p,q)$-forms on a complex manifold $M$ is  defined as the section space of the vector bundles
  \[
    \bigwedge^{p,q}T^{*}M=\bigwedge^{p}T^{*}M\otimes_{\co}\bigwedge^{q}T^{*}M,
  \]
  \noindent and will be denoted as $\Omega^{(p,q)}(M)$, and the following \emph{``conjugation''} convention is asumed: $\overline{\om^{(p,q)}(M)}=\om^{(q,p)}(M)$, with the space of complex differential forms given as the space
  \[
    \om^{k}_{\co}(M)=\bigoplus_{p+q=k}\om^{p,q}(M).
  \]

  The external differential operator is $d=\partial + \overline{\partial}$, where $\partial:\om^{(p,q)}(M)\rightarrow \om^{(p+1,q)}(M)$ is the holomorphic differential operator and $\overline{\partial}:\om^{(p,q)}(M)\rightarrow\om^{(p,q+1)}(M)$ is the anti-holomorphic differential operator. Similarly, there is the ``twisted'' or ``conjugated'' differential operator $d^c=i(\overline{\partial}-\partial)$, I will use both operators thoughout this thesis, but more important for this work is the related Kähler operator which will be usedd extensively in various ways
  \[
    \frac{i}{2}dd^c\varphi=i\partial\overline{\partial}\varphi\quad\varphi:M\to\re,\varphi\in\csheaf^2{M}.
  \]

  Unless stated otherwise a submanifold will be understood as an embedded submanifold, i.e. $N$ is an embedded (differential/complex) submanifold of $M$ if $N$ is a (differential/complex) manifold such that there exist $f:N\rightarrow M$ a (differental/holomorphic) immersion.

  \section{Analytic and Algebraic Varieties}
  \noindent The manifolds and varieties that will be studied in this thesis are complex and holomorphic which has important consequences for the ring of local functions. For starters, for any $z\in U\subset\con$ open subset, the ring $\osheaf_{z}$ is isomorphic to the set of \emph{convergent power series arround the point $z$.} Moreover, the ring $\osheaf_{n}$ is an integral domain for all $n\in\nat$, there is also \emph{Weierstrass' preparation and division Theorems} where there is alot more we can know about $\osheaf_{n}$ by looking at the ring of polynomials of the ring of holomorphic germs at a lesser dimesnion $\osheaf_{n-1}$, since there are a natural inclusions
  \[
    \osheaf_{n-1}\xhookrightarrow{}\osheaf_{n-1}[z_{n}]\xhookrightarrow{}\osheaf_{n}.
  \]
  Where $z_{n}$ clearly denotes the nth coordinate in $\con$. In order to study $\osheaf_{n}$ in terms of $\osheaf_{n-1}$ the notion is necesary to know how a $f\in\osheaf_{n}$ behaves in terms of $z_{n}$, we say $f\in\osheaf_{n}$ is \emph{regular of order $k$ in $z_{n}$} if $g(z_{n})=f(0,\dots,0,z_{n})$ has a cero of order $k$ at $0$.
  \begin{theorem}{Weierstrass's Preparation Theorem}\label{weier-prep}
  If $f\in\osheaf_{n}$ is regular of order $k$ in $z_{n}$, then there is a unique \emph{Weiertrass polynomial} $h\in\osheaf[z_{n}]$ of degree $k$ such that $f=uh$, for some unit $u\in\osheaf_{n}$.
  \end{theorem}
  Where a \emph{Weiertrass polynomial} of degree $k>0$ in $z_{n}$ is polynomial $h\in\osheaf_{n-1}[z_{n}]$ such that
  \[
    h(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})=z_{n}^{k}+\alpha_{1}(z_{1}\dots,z_{n-1})z_{n}^{k-1}+\dots+\alpha_{k}(z_{1},\dots,z_{n-1}).
  \]
  With $\alpha_{j}\in\osheaf_{n-1}$ are \emph{nonunits}. In a similar way there is
  \begin{theorem}{Weierstrass's Division Theorem}\label{weier-div}
    Let $h\in\osheaf_{n-1}[z_{n}]$ be a \emph{Weierstrass polynoial in $z_{n}$} of degree $k$. Then for any $f\in\osheaf_{n}$ there are $g\in\osheaf_{n}$ and a polynomial $r\in\osheaf_{n-1}[z_{n}]$ of degree $<k$ such that $f=gh+r$. Moreover if $f\in\osheaf_{n-1}[z_{n}]$, then $g\in\osheaf_{n-1}[z_{n}]$.
  \end{theorem}
  See \cite{Gunning-Rossi}. Now, as the art of algebraic geometry goes, the main focus of study will be \emph{varieties}, which is the \emph{geometric counterpart} of rings and ideals as the \emph{Nullstellensatz theorem} indicates. For a set of functions $A\subset\osheaf_{U}$, where $U\subset M$ is an open set on a complex manifold, the vanishing set of is denoted and defined as
  \[
    V(A):=\{x\in U\,\vert\,f(x)=0,\,\forall\,f\in A\}.
  \]
  \noindent Conversely the \emph{ideal defined by a set $V\subset\con$} is denoted and defined as
  \[
    \mathcal{I}(V):=\{f\in\osheaf_{n}\,|\,f|_{V}\equiv0\}.
  \]
  \begin{definition}[Analytic Variety]
    An \emph{analytic variety} is defined is a paracompact Hausdorff space $X$ with a locally ringed structure $\osheaf_{X}$ modeled by the local ring structure of a vanishing set in some finite dimensional complex vector space. That is, $\osheaf_{X}$ is a sheaf of rings on $X$ such that for every $x\in X$ there is a open neighborhood of $x$, $U_{x}$ and a isomorphism of ringed spaces to a local model $(Z,\osheaf(W)/(f_{1},\dots,f_{k}))$, meaning a pair $(\varphi,\phi)$ where $W\subset\con$ is and open set, $\varphi:U_{x}\rightarrow W\cap Z$ is an homeomorphism and $\phi=\varphi_{*}:\osheaf(W)/(f_{1},\dots,f_{k})\rightarrow\osheaf_{X}(U_{x})$ is a sheaf isomorphism. Moreover $Z=V(f_{1},\dots,f_{k})$, and $(f_{1},\dots,f_{k})$ denotes the ideal of $\osheaf(W)$ generated by the functions $\{f_{1},\dots,f_{k}\}$.
  \end{definition}
  \noindent Given a open set $V\subset X$, an open set $U\subset\co$ and an isomorphism between ringed structures as previously discussed, $\varphi:V\rightarrow U\cap V(f_{1},\dots,f_{k})$, the pair $(V,\varphi)$ will be called a \emph{chart}.

  The definition of a complex algebraic variety is the same as the definition of a complex analytic variety, the only difference is that instead of the ring of germs of holomorphic functions, the ring of polynomials $\osheaf=\co[z_{1},\dots,z_{n}]$ is used as the structure ring. Moreover an analytic or algebraic variety $X$ is said to be of pure dimension $k$ if all of its irreducible components are of the same dimension $k$.

  An \emph{analytic subspace} of an analytic variety $(X,\osheaf_{X})$ consist of a subset $Y\subset X$ such that for every $y\in Y$ there is a chart $(U,\varphi)$ around $y$ with
  \[
    \varphi(Y\cap U)=V_{\varphi(U)}(\varphi_{*}(\eta_1),\dots,\varphi_{*}(\eta_k)),
  \]

  \noindent and $\lbrace\eta_1,\dots,\eta_k\rbrace\subset\osheaf_{n}$, naturally we have a local ring structure $(Y,\osheaf_{Y})$ given by $\osheaf_Y:=\osheaf_{X}/\mathcal{I}(Y)$, as before and the definition of a \emph{algebraic subspace} can be decuced by replacing the stucture ring by the ring of polynomials, the terms analytic set, analytic subset and analytic variety, analytic subvariety are going to be used interchangeable

  Of particular interest are the analytic and algebraic subset of the \emph{complex projective space} $\cp^{n}$, note that using projective coordinates, the set of vanishing points of \emph{homogeneous} polynomials are well defined on $\cp^n$ and therefore algebraic projective subsets exist for $\cp^{n}$. Analytic and algebraic subsets of $\cp^n$ are going to be called \emph{\textbf{projective} analytic subsets} and \emph{\textbf{projective} algebraic subsets} respectively.

  If $X$ is a purely $k$-dimensional analytic subset of $U\subset\con$, the singular locus of $X$, $\sig(X)$ is the set of points where the defining functions of $X$, say $X\cap U=V(f_{1},\dots,f_{n-k})$ have derivatives that span a space of dimension \emph{less than $k$}, meaning that the matrix defined by the partial derivatives
  \[
    M=\left(\dfrac{\partial f_{j}}{\partial z_{i}}\right)_{ij}
  \]
  has a rank \emph{less than $k$}. Therefore, given an analytic set $X$ of an open set $U$, the analytic set of regular points of $X$ is $X_{reg}:=X\setminus \sig(X)\subset U':=U\setminus \sig(X)$, The set $U'$ is said to be the open subset of regular points of $U$.

  \subsection{Stein Spaces and Coherent Analytic Sheaves}
  Similarly, spaces with particularly important and well known propperties area the so called \emph{Stein Spaces}, which are complex analytic subspaces of $\con$ that generalizes the notion of domains of holomophy in $\con$ to singular complex spaces.

  A \emph{Stein space} is an analytic space $(X,\osheaf_X)$ that is embeddable as an analytic subspace to $\con$ for some $n$ and is holomorphically convex and separable. A domain $U\subset\con$ is \emph{holomorphically convex} if for every proper subset $K\subset U$ the holomorphic hull
  \[
    \hat{K}_U=\{z\in U\,|\,|f(z)|\leq\sup_{w\in K}\, , \,\forall\,f\in\osheaf(U)\}
  \]
  is a proper subset of U. Holomorphic convex domains are the setting for spaces with sufficiently rich rings of germs of holomorphic functions, in the same spirit, a complex analytic space $X$ is \emph{holomorphically separable} if for all $x\neq y$ in $X$, there is a $f\in\osheaf_X$ such that $f(x)\neq f(y)$. This means that Stein spaces have a healthy amount of holomorphic functions, making their function theory non-trivial. 

  With a sufficiently rich ring of germs holomoprhic functions of a complex analytic space $X$, there are natural consequences about the sheaves of $\osheaf_X$-modules on them
  \begin{definition}
    A sheaf of abelian groups on a topological space $(X,\tau_X)$ is a contravariant functor $\fsheaf:\tau_X\to\mathrm{Ab}$ where the categorial structure of $\tau_X$ is the categorial structure of a partial ordered set and $s|_V$ denotes the image of an element $s\in\fsheaf(U)$ to a smaller subset $V\subset U$. Furthemore $\fsheaf$ satifies the following conditions

    \begin{itemize}
    \item If $U$ is an open set and $\{W_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\Lambda}$ is an open covering of $U$, then if $s\in\fsheaf(U)$ is such that $s|_{W_{\alpha}}=0$ for all $\alpha\in\Lambda$ then $s=0$.       
    \item If $U$ is an open set and $\{W_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\Lambda}$ is an open covering of $U$, then if there is a $s_{\alpha}\in\fsheaf(W_{\alpha})$ for each $\alpha\in\Lambda$, such that $s_{\alpha}|_{W_{\alpha}\cap W_{\beta}}=s_{\beta}|_{W_{\alpha}\cap W_{\beta}}$ for all $\{\alpha,\beta\}\subset\Lambda$ then there is a $s\in\fsheaf(U)$ with $s|_{W_{\alpha}}=s_{\alpha}$. 
    \end{itemize}
    \end{definition}
    For analytic spaces the most common to study are sheaves of $\osheaf_X$-modues, morever sheaves are closed under common algebraic functorial constructs like, products, sums, tensors, limits, etc, therefore, I will be making free use of such constructs of sheaves. For a sheaf of abelian groups
    $\fsheaf$, the \emph{stalk} at a point $p$ denoted as $\fsheaf_p$ is the limit group under $\fsheaf(U)\to\fsheaf(V)$ with $V\subset U$ over all open sets containing $p$.

  A very important approach to the study of analytic varieties is to study is to focus on \emph{coherent and quasicoherent} sheaves of $\osheaf_X$ modules of an analytic and algebraic varieties as showed by Serre with its GAGA theorems. A sheaf of $\osheaf_X$-modules in $X$ is \emph{quasicoherent} if for every point in $X$ there is an open neighborhood $U$ and sets $I$ and $J$ such that
    \[
      \osheaf^{(I)}_X(U)\to\osheaf^{(J)}_X(U)\to\fsheaf(U)
    \]
  is an  exact sequence. In this vain there is a more restrictive type of sheaf are \emph{coherent sheaves}
  \begin{definition}
    A sheaf $\fsheaf$ of $\osheaf_X$-modules on a complex space $X$ is coherent if it satifies the following two properties
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $\fsheaf$ is of finite type, meaning that locally, for any point in $X$ there is a neighborhoof $U$ and a local epimorphism $\osheaf^{n}_X(U)\to\fsheaf(U)$ for some $n\in\nat$.
    \item For any $n\in\nat$ and any morphism of $\osheaf_X$-modules, $\varphi:\osheaf^{n}_X\to\fsheaf$ its kernel is also of finite type.
  \end{itemize}
  \end{definition}
  For coherent sheaves of $\osheaf_X$-modules on \emph{Stein} spaces, Cartan's theorems A and B show that the stalk $\fsheaf_p$ is generated by the global sections and that their sheaf comhomology groups vanish for al orders greater than $1$. 

  \begin{theorem}[Cartan theorems A and B]\label{CartanAB}
    Let $X$ be a reduced Stein space and $\fsheaf$ a coherent analytic sheaf on $X$, then the following are Cartan's theorems A and B respectively:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item[A:]$\fsheaf_p$ is generated by the global  sections of $\fsheaf$ for all $p\in\fsheaf$.
    \item[B:]$H^q(X,\fsheaf)=0$ for all $q\geq 1$
  \end{itemize}
    \end{theorem}
  A polydisk $\Delta^n=\{z\in\con\,|\,|z_j|<r_j,\quad j\in\{1,\dots,n\}\}$ is a convex bounded domain of $\con$, hence its pseudoconvex, and therefore Stein. Consequently, Cartan's theorems apply to any coherent analytic sheaf restricted to any polydisk $\Delta^n$ i.e. every such sheaf is generated at each stalk by its sections over and all higher cohomology vanishes. This local applicability is of fundamental importance in the global theory; any complex manifold $X$ admits a cover by Stein open sets (polydisks), thus given a canonical cover by polydisks in local coordinates, the vanishing of higher cohomology on each element of such a cover (a \emph{Leray cover}), allows one to compute the sheaf cohomology of $X$.
  \section{Kähler Manifolds}
  There are several equivalent ways to define a K\"ahler manifold, depending on the problem one is tackling, the traditonal way to define a Kähler manifold is as a complex manifold $(M,J)$ with an Hermitian structure $h=g-i\omega$ such that the (1,1)-form defined by $\omega=-\Im (h)$ is closed $d\,\omega=0$. This closed form is denoted as the \emph{K\"ahler form}, this means that a Kähler manifold $M$ is a real differentiable manifold of dimension $2d$ with a \emph{symplectic stucture} and an complex structure, i.e. $J:TM\rightarrow TM,\quad J^2=-Id$ is such that the Schouten–Nijenhuis bracket vanishes
  \[
    N_{J}(X,Y)=[X,Y]+J[JX,Y]+J[X,JY]-[JX,JY]=0.
  \]
  However this is not enough as there are complex manifolds with symplectic structure that are not Kähler, so Kähler manifolds are special in that the symplectic structure comes from an Hermitian product $h$ on the tangent space of $M$. Therefore Kähler maifolds are a more restrictive class of manifolds than the class of complex symplectic manifolds even with more strict topological restrictions such as compacity and simple connectedness (see \ref{Baz-Vic})

  \begin{definition}
          Let $M$ be a complex manifold with with respect to the integrable complex structure $J:TM\rightarrow TM$. A \textit{Hermitian metric} on $M$ is a smooth family real bilinear forms $\lbrace h_p\rbrace_p$ for all $p\in M$ where $h_p:T_pM\times T_pM\rightarrow\co$, such that
  \begin{itemize}
          \item $h_p(JX,JY)=h_p(X,Y)\hspace{0.3cm}\forall\lbrace X,Y\rbrace\subset T_pM\hspace{0.3cm}\forall p\in M.$
          \item $h_p(X,JX)>0\hspace{0.3cm}\forall X\in T_p M\setminus\lbrace 0\rbrace\hspace{0.3cm}\forall p\in M.$
          \item $h_p(X,Y)=\overline{h_p(Y,X)}\hspace{0.3cm}\forall\lbrace X,Y\rbrace\subset T_pM\hspace{0.3cm}\forall p\in M.$
  \end{itemize}
  An Hermitian metric $h$ will also be denoted as $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$, a manifold with a Hermitian metric $(M,h)$ its called a \textit{Hermitian manifold}.
  \end{definition}
  An Hermitian metric $h$ has an associated Riemannian metric and a associated $(1,1)$-form given by the real and imaginary part of $h$ respectively. Conversely, a Riemannian manifold of even dimension $(M,g)$ with an almost complex structure $J$ has a natural Hermitian metric given by
  \[
    h(X,Y):=g(X,Y)-i\omega(X,Y).
  \]
  \noindent Where $\omega$ is its associated 2-form given by $\omega(X,Y)=g(JX,Y)$ which is clearly antisymmetric since $J^2=-Id_M$. From the geometric standpoint this means that Hermitian manifolds and Hermitian structures are special because of the relationship between their Riemannian metrics and their associated $(1,1)$-forms, one aspect of this relationship is the fact that both volume forms coincide, meaning that $g$ is a Riemannian metric with the same volume form as the one induced by $\omega$.

  \begin{definition}\label{Kahler-metric}
          Let $(M,h)$ be a Hermitian manifold with Hermitian metric $h$ and associated $(1,1)$-form $\omega$, $h$ is a Kähler metric if
          \[
            d\omega=0,
          \]
          \noindent an Hermitian manifold with a Kähler metric is called a \textit{Kähler manifold}, and its associated $(1,1)$-form is  called a \textit{Kähler form} or a \textit{Kähler symplectic form}.
  \end{definition}

  The most important prototypical examples of K\"ahler manifolds are the complex vector space of dimension $n$, $\con$ with the traditional Hermitian inner product and the more interesting family of projective spaces $\cp^{n}$ with the metric known as the Fubini-Study metric. Moreover a smooth projective variety is a Kähler manifold and the converse does hold when $[\omega]\in H^{2}(M,\zah)$ by Kodaira's theorem \ref{Kod1}. I will use the complex projective space and their submanifolds and analytic subsets \emph{extensively} throughout this theis.

  \begin{example}
    The complex projetive space of dimesion $n$ $\cp^{n}$ is a Kähler manifold with the Fubini-Study metric, which in homogeneous coordinates $Z=[1:\zeta_{1}:\dots:\zeta_{n}]=[1:\zeta]$ is defined by the Kähler form
    \[
      \omega_{FS}=\frac{i}{2}\partial \overline{\partial}\log(|Z|^{2})=\frac{i}{2}\partial \overline{\partial}\log(1+|\zeta|^{2}).
    \]
  \end{example}

  Likewise, it is important to mention that every complex submanifold of a Kähler manifold is Kähler. Similarly, compact complex submanifolds of a Kähler manifold minimize the volume in their homology class. This is some evidence that Kähler manifolds have some strong topological restrictions, another way to express this is the fact that powers of the Kähler form $\omega^k$ are non trivial representatives of cohomology classes in $H^{2k}(M ;\re)$, meaning these groups are never trivial. More importantly complex submanifolds of a Kähler manifold are also Kähler.

  \subsection{Kähler manifolds and their volumes}
  Being a Kähler manifold has some strong topological restrictions. For example, powers of the K\"ahler form $\omega^k$ are nontrivial representatives of cohomology classes in $H^{2k}(M ;\re)$, meaning that these groups are never trivial.
  More important for our purposes is the fact that, by our definition of submanifold, it follows that every complex submanifold (or immersed complex submanifold) of a K\"ahler manifold is also Kähler since the closedness of the 2-form follows from the compatibility of $J$ and the derivative under the pullback $f^*$. Other consequences are the following equivalent results, known as ``Wirtinger's inequality'', which imply that Kähler manifolds have a special of geometry, a type geometry known as \emph{calibrated geometry}, later I will expand on this, but Wirtinger's inequality provides an important link between complex geometry and strucutes and calibrated geometries and structures

  \begin{theorem}[Wirtinger's inequality]Let $V$  be a real vector space  of even dimension $2k$ endowed with a positive-definite inner product $g$, symplectic form $\omega$, and  an almost-complex structure $J$  linked, as before by $\omega(u, v) = g(J(u), v)\, ,\forall\,u,v\in{V}$. Then for any orthonormal vectors $u_1, \dots, u_{2k}$ the following inequality holds:
    \[
      (\underbrace {\omega \wedge \cdots \wedge \omega }_{k{\text{ times}}})(u_{1},\ldots ,u_{2k})\leq k!.
    \]
  There is equality if and only if the span of $u_1,\dots, u_{2k}$ is closed under the action of $J$.
  \end{theorem}
  As a consequence for the volume forms for submanifolds of K\"ahler manifolds we have the following
  \begin{theorem}[Complex submanifolds of Kähler manifolds minimize volume]
  Let $M$ be a K\"ahler manifold with K\"ahler form $\omega_M$ and let $f:N\rightarrow M$ be a closed and oriented immersion
  of an oriented real manifold of real dimension $2k$. Let $\omega=f^*\,\omega_m$, then
  \begin{equation}
              \int_{N} \frac{\omega^k}{k!}\leq \int_N d\textrm{Vol}_N\hspace{0.3cm}\text{where }d\textrm{Vol}_N\text{ is the volume form of N,}
  \end{equation}
  and the equality holds if and only if $N$ is a complex submanifold of $M$.
  \end{theorem}
  We can state the natural conclusions as the following theorem
  \begin{theorem}\label{wirtinger}
          Let $N$ be a complex compact submanifold of a K\"ahler manifold $M$, then $N$ is a volume-minimizing submanifold in its homology class $H_{2k}(M,\partial N,\zah)$, meaning that for any real submanifold $X$ of real dimension $2k$ that is homologous to $N$, has a greater volume than $N$, meaning
          \[
            \mathrm{Vol}_{2k}(N)\leq \mathrm{Vol}_{2k}(X).
          \]
  \end{theorem}
  Given $(M,g)$ a oriented Riemannian manifold of dimension $n$, then $M$ has a natural volume form $\textrm{Vol}_{n}$. In local coordinates, is expressed as
  \[
  \textrm{Vol}_{n}=\sqrt{|g|}dx_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge dx_{n}.
  \]
  Let $\omega=-Im\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ be the standard 2-form of the standard Euclidean K\"ahler metric in $\con$ and let $M$ be a complex submanifold of $\con$ by Wirtinger's inequality, if $\textrm{Vol}_{2k}(M)$ is the volume of $M$ given by the Riemannian structure as before, $g=Re\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\vert_{M}$, then
  \begin{equation}
          \textrm{Vol}_{2k}(M)= \frac{1}{k!}\int_M \omega^k.
  \end{equation}

  If $X$ is a purely $k$-dimensional analytic subset of $\con$, then by definition, the singular locus of $X$, $\sig(X)$ is an analytic subset of lesser dimension than $X$, therefore $M=X\setminus\sig(X)$ is a $k$ dimensional complex manifold and the volume of $\sig(X)$ is negligible, i.e.
  \[
      \textrm{Vol}_{2k}(X)= \frac{1}{k!}\int_{X} \omega^k= \frac{1}{k!}\int_{X\setminus\sig(X)} \omega^k=
      \textrm{Vol}_{2k}(X\setminus\sig(X)).
  \]


  \section{Calibrated manifolds}
  Now I would like to address \emph{calibrations and calibrated geometries}, a calibrated geometry is a way to describe a geometry via a distinguished family of submanifolds, more precisely, a volume minimizing family of subsets. This approach can be better suited for understanding \emph{foliated geometries}, as I will show later, Bishop's theorems is a very valuable too that help us with such an endeavor.
  In order to distinguish said family of subsets, a \emph{calibration} is needed, a calibration can be thought of a way to find volume minimizing manifolds of certain dimensions (see \cite{Harvey}). K\"ahler manifolds and their submanifolds have a natural calibration by Wirtinger's inequality. The relationship between the symplectic and volume forms of a Hermitian inner product and the Riemannian volume implies that the normalized exterior powers of the Kähler form of a Kähler manifold are calibrations.

  \begin{definition}[Calibrated manifolds] A \textbf{calibrated $n$-manifold} is a Riemannian manifold $(M, g)$ of dimension $n$ equipped with a differential $p$-form $\varphi\,$  ($0 \leq {p} \leq{n}$) with the following properties
  \begin{enumerate}
  \item $\varphi$ is closed, meaning $d\varphi = 0$, where $d$ is the exterior derivative.
  \item For any $x \in M$ and any oriented $p$-dimensional subspace $\xi$ of $T_{x}M$, $\varphi|_{\xi} = \lambda \textrm{Vol}_{\xi}$ with $\lambda\leq 1$.
  \end{enumerate}
  When referring to Riemannian manifolds, $\textrm{Vol}_{\xi}$ denotes the volume form of  $\xi$ with respect to the Riemannian metric $g$. The $p$-form its called a $p$-\emph{calibration}.
  \end{definition}
  For $x\in M$, set $G_{x}(\varphi) = \{ \xi \subset T_{x}M\,\vert\,  \varphi|_{\xi} = \textrm{Vol}_{\xi} \}$. In order for the theory to be nontrivial, we need $G_x(\varphi)$ to be nonempty. Let $G(\varphi)$ denote the union of all $G_x(\varphi)$ with $x\in M$.
  \begin{definition}[Calibrated submanifold]
  A $p$-dimensional calibrated submanifold of a manifold $M$ with calibration $\varphi$ is an oriented submanifold $\sig$ such that the calibration restricted to the tangent bundle of $\Sigma$, equals the induced volume form of the submanifold $\varphi|_{T\Sigma}=\textrm{Vol}_{\Sigma}$. Equivalently $T\Sigma\subset G(\varphi)$
  \end{definition}
  For calibrated manifolds and submanifolds the volume-minimizing property of submanifolds in the same homological class is proven by the following one line argument
  \begin{equation}\label{oneline}
    \int_{\sig}\mathrm{Vol}_{\Sigma}=\int_{\Sigma}\varphi =\int_{\Sigma'}\varphi \leq \int_{\Sigma'}\mathrm{Vol}_{\Sigma'}
  \end{equation}
  where the first equality holds because $\sig$ is calibrated, the second equality is Stoke's theorem (as $\varphi$ is closed), and the last inequality holds because $ \varphi$ is a calibration.
  Here $\sig$ is a calibrated submanifold of $M$ and $\Sigma'$ is any submanifold in the same homology class of $\Sigma$. Note that a Kähler manifold $(M,\omega)$ is a calibrated manifold with its calibrations given by the Kähler form and its powers $\{\omega^{k}\,\vert\, 1\leq k\leq \dim(M)\}$. Meaning that $(M,\omega^{k})$ is a calibrated manifold for every $k\in\{1,\dots,\dim(M)\}$, and the previous one line argument is a version of Wirtinger's inequality. There are more aspects of calibrated geometries thet will be explored later, the aspects of calibrations that are most important for this thesis are the ones related to \emph{foliated geometries} and \emph{integration currents.}

  \section{The Hausdorff measure and The Hausdorff metric}
  \noindent One way to realate the geometric structure of analytic sets or manifolds and their analytic structure will be through a very useful tool that is directly related to the volumes, the Hausdorff measure, which can be conceptualized as a generalization of the volume for arbitrary dimensions and is in itself a tool for measuring dimensions as an arbitrary non-negative real number for arbitrary metric spaces $(X,\rho)$. First some notation, for a set on a \textbf{bounded} set $S\subset X$ the diamater is denoted as
  \[
  \textrm{diam}(S)=\max\{\rho(x,y)\,|\,x,y\in S\}
  \]
  \begin{definition}
  Let $S$ be any subset of $X$ and any $\delta\in\re^{+}$. The Hausdorff measure of dimension $d$ bounded by $\delta$ is defined as
  \[
  H_{\delta}^{d}(S)=\inf\Big\{\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{diam}(U_i)^{d}\,|\,S\subset\bigcup_{i=0}^{\infty}U_i,\,\mathrm{diam}(U_i)<\delta\Big\},
  \]
  \end{definition}
  \noindent where the infimum is taken over all the countable covers of $S$ with sets of diameter less than $\delta$. With this, the \emph{$d$-Hausdorff outer measure of dimension $d$} also known as the \emph{Hausdorff measure of dimension $d$}, is defined as the limit of the increasing sequence $\{H_{\delta}^d\}$ when $\delta\rightarrow 0$, in other words
  \[
  \mathcal{H}^d(S):=\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}H_{\delta}^d(S).
  \]
  The $d$-Hausdorff measure is a rough measure of the dimensionality of the set being measured and in the case of analytic sets it IS a measure of the dimensionality of a analytic subset. Note here that the definition does not specify that $d\in\nat$ and in fact the Hausdorff dimension may be any real $d\in\re$, but in the case of analytic and algebraic sets, the dimension is a natural number and coincides with the notion of dimension of analytic and algebraic varieties. Here I would like to mention in a brief section, that in general in the history of mathematics there is a rich discussion about te notion of \emph{dimension} of certan object of study, its not always inmediatly obvious how to define it, for example in this thesis there are two ways for assigning a dimension to a complex manifold/variety one is the ``real diemension'' and the other is the ``complex dimension'', here there is not such a big discrepancy since the two are related in many ways and one is two times the other but never the less its interesting, see \cite{YURI-MANIM:ON-THE-NOTION-OF-DIMENSION..}).

  \noindent Here is a list some of the most important properties of the Hausdorff measure for the purpose of this thesis, see \cite{Stolzenberg}[ch. 3]:

  \begin{enumerate}
          \item[1.] If $\mathcal{H}^d(S)<\infty$, and $d<k$, then $\mathcal{H}^k(S)=0$.\\

          \item[2.] If $f:X\rightarrow Y$ is a Lipschitz continuous function with
          Lipschitz constant $\lambda$, then for any $d\in\re^{+}$ and
          $S\subset X$, the following inequality holds
          \[
          \mathcal{H}^d(f(S))\leq\lambda^{\delta}\mathcal{H}^d(S).
          \]
          \item[3.]If $X=\re^{n}$ and $S$ is a smooth submanifold of dimension
          $k\in\zah^{+}$, then the volume of $S$ as a submanifold is related to
          its Hausdorff measure by the formula
          \[
          \textrm{Vol}_{k}(M)=\alpha_{k}\mathcal{H}^k(S),\hspace*{0.3cm}\alpha_k= \frac{\pi^k}{\Gamma(k/2+1)},
          \]
  \noindent where $\Gamma(z)$ is the Euler's gamma function.
  \end{enumerate}
  Similarly, if the Hausdorff measure is a measure of dimension, the Hausdorff metric is a way to measure how close two closed sets are
  \begin{definition}
  Let $(X,\rho)$ be a metric space and $A,B$ two subsets of $X$, the Hausdorff metric defined for $A$
  and $B$ is defined as
  \[
  d_H(A,B):= \max\Big\{\sup_{a\in A}d(a,B),\sup_{b\in B}d(b,B)\Big\}.
  \]
  \end{definition}
  The Hausdorff metric allows to define a convenient notion of convergence of closed subsets; let $\{S_n \}$ be any sequence of closed subsets of $X$, then $S_n$ converges to the closed subset $S$ in the Hausdorff metric $S_n\overset{h}\rightarrow S$, if for every $K\subset X$ compact we have
  \[
    d_H(K\cap S_n,K\cap S)\rightarrow 0.
  \]
  \noindent This convergence is a particular version of the Gromov-Hausdorff convergence, however for analytic varieties it is enough to work with Hausdorff convergence instead of the more complex theory of Gromov-Hausdorff convergence.
  The Gromov-Hausdorff metric is defined as the \emph{infimum} of all the Hausdorff distances of isometric embeddings of any two bounded metric spaces, meaning that the Gromov-Hausdorff distance does not need an ambient space to measure the distance beetween two spaces, more formally
  \begin{definition}[Gromov-Hausdorff distance]\label{Gromov-Hausdorff}
    Let $(X,d_{x})$ and $(Y,d_{Y})$ be bounded metric spaces, the Gromov-Hausdorff distance between them is defined as
    \begin{equation}
      d_{GH}(X,Y):=\inf d_{H}(\phi(X),\psi(Y)).
    \end{equation}
    Where the infimum is taken over all isometric embeddings $\phi$, $\psi$ into a metric space $Z$ and over all metric spaces $Z$.
  \end{definition}
  Being the infimum, implies that any sequence of closed sets of some ambient metric space that is convergent in the Hausdorff metric, is convergent in the Gromov-Hausdorff metric, and that is why here only Hausdorff convergence will be considered. Its worth mentioning that Gromov-Hausdorff convergence has been used in recent years for the study of holomorphic vector bundles on Kähler manifolds with particular curvature considerations, in particular, Donadlson, Tiean, Sun et al have used Gromov-Hausdorff convergence for studying the Ricci curvatures of Kähler-Einstein manifolds, their convergence results share some resembrance to the ones I expose here and its worth asking about how far this similarities can bie taken (see \cite{Donadlson-Sun} and \cite{tian}) I will explore this in the end section and in the future.

  \section{Plurisuhbharmonic functions}
  \noindent Plurisubharmonicfunctions appear constantly in the analytic study of Kähler metrics and their curvatures and volumes, they were independently intoduced by both Lelong and Oka in the study of holomorphic convexity.
  \begin{definition}\label{psh}
    A funtion \(\varphi:U\to\re\) defined on an open subset \(U\subset\con\), is said to be plurisubharmonic (often shortened as \textbf{psh}) if \(\varphi\) is upper semi continuous and if for every complex line
    \[
      L=\{\alpha_0 z+\beta_0\,|\,\,z\in\co,\text{ with }\{\alpha_0,\beta_0\}\subset\con\text{ fixed }\}.
    \]
    The restiction of \(\varphi|_{U\cap L}\) is subharmonic, meaning that
    \begin{equation}
      \varphi(w)\leq\int_0^{2\pi}\varphi(w+e^{i\theta}\zeta)\,d\theta
    \end{equation}
    Where \(\zeta\in\con\) is small enough to have \(w+e^{i\theta}\zeta\in U\). The space of plurisubharmonic functions on \(U\) will be denoted as \(\psh(U)\).
  \end{definition}
  The inequality in the previous definitions is known as the \emph{mean value inequality}. Plurisubharmonic functions have a lot of properties, here are some of the most basic properrties of them
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Every function \(\varphi\in\psh\) is subharmonic, namely, the \emph{mean value inequality} on spheres/balls is valid for \(\varphi\):
          \begin{equation}
            \varphi(\zeta)\leq\frac{n!}{\pi^nr^{2n}}\int_{B_r(\zeta)}\varphi(z)\,d\lambda(z)
          \end{equation}
      for every  \(\zeta\in U\) and \(B_r(\zeta)\subset U\) open ball.
    \item For any decreasing sequence \(\{\varphi_n\}_{n\in\nat}\subset\psh(U)\), the limit function
          \[
          \varphi=\lim_{n\to\infty}\varphi_n=\min_{n\in\nat}\{\varphi_n\},
          \]
      is psh.
    \item Let \(\{\phi_1,\dots,\phi_k\}\subset\psh(U)\) and \(f:\re^k\to\re\) be a convex funtion such that \(f(t_1,\dots,t_k)\) is increasing in each \(t_j\). Then \(f(\phi_1,\dots,\phi_k)\in\psh(U)\), in particular, \(\phi_1+\dots+\phi_k\in\psh(U)\), \(\max\{\phi_1,\dots,\phi_k\}\in\psh(U)\) and \(\log(e^{\phi_1}+\dots+e^{\phi_k})\in\psh(U)\).
    \item \(\varphi\in\csheaf^2(U)\) is psh if and only if the Hermitian form
      \begin{equation}
        H\varphi(\zeta)[\xi]=\sum_{1\leq j,k\leq n}\dfrac{\partial^2\varphi(\zeta)}{\partial z_j\partial\overline{z}_k}\,\xi_j\overline{\xi}_k,
      \end{equation}
          is positive semi-definite for all \(\zeta\in U\). Moreover \(H\varphi[\xi]\) defines a positive measure for all \(\xi\), note that in the case of holomorphic manifolds, \(H\varphi\) coincides with \(i\dd\varphi\).
  \end{enumerate}

  \noindent A plurisubharmonic function $\varphi:U\to\re$ is said to be \emph{\textbf{strictly plurisubharmonic functions}} if the previous inequalities are strict. Furthermore, analogously to plurisubharmonic functions, a \emph{\textbf{pluriharmonic function}} is a twice differentiable function \(f:U\to\re\) defined on an open subset \(U\subset\con\) such that for every complex line
    \[
      L=\{\alpha_0 z+\beta_0\,|\,\,z\in\co,\text{ with }\{\alpha_0,\beta_0\}\subset\con\text{ fixed }\}.
    \]
    The restiction of \(f|_{U\cap L}\) is harmonic $\nabla^2f(w)=0$. Equivalently for complex manifols, a real valued function \hbox{$f:M\to\re$} is pluriharmonic if $dd^{c}f=0$. See \cite{Gunning-Rossi}[pp. 271].

  \section{Holomorphic Foliations}

  \begin{definition}{Foliation}
  A foliation $\mathfrak{F}$ on a manifold $M$ of real dimension $n$, can be defined in a numerous of distict and interesting ways, depending on the application needed a foliation will be understood as
  \begin{itemize}
    \item A decomposition of $M$ into disjoint connected submanifolds called \emph{leaves},
          \[
            M=\bigcup_{\alpha}\mathcal{L_{\alpha}},
          \]
          \noindent such that for every point $p\in M$ there exists an open set and a ($\mathcal{C}^{k}$, differential, analytic or holomorphic) function $(U,\phi)$, with $\phi\rightarrow\re^{m}$, such that every $\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}\cap U$ is a level set of $\phi$.
    \item A \emph{foliated chart} $\{(U_{i},\varphi_{i})\}$ such that $\{U_{i}\}$ is a covering of $M$ and $\varphi_{i}:U_{i}\rightarrow\re^{n}$ is a ($\mathcal{C}^{k}$, differential, analytic or holomorphic) chart such that
          \[
            \varphi_{i}(U_{i})=W_{i}\times\re^{m},\text{ with }W_{i}\subset\re^{n-m}.
          \]
        \item An \emph{involutive} distribution of the tangent bundle $TM$, i.e. A $\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(M)$-module $\mathfrak{F}\subset\mathcal{T}M$ closed under the lie bracket
          \[
            [X,Y]\in\mathfrak{F}\text{ for any } \{X,Y\}\subset\mathfrak{F}
          \]
    \item The dual poin of view from the previous viewpoint will also be adopted, i.e. a foliation $\mathfrak{F}$ is a set of $k$-forms that nulls on an involutive disribution.
  \end{itemize}

  If all the leaves are of the same dimension, then the foliation is said to be \emph{regular}, note that a foliated chart determines the dimension of the leaves. This is equivalent to all the spaces generated by the pointwise evaluations of $\mathfrak{F}$ being of the same dimension.
  \end{definition}

  \begin{definition}
  The \emph{holonomy grupoid} also called the \emph{graph} of a foliation $(M,\mathfrak{F})$ is the set of all curves contained in the leaves of the foliation \textit{modulo homotopy} and will be denoted as
  \[
  G(M,\mathfrak{F}):=\{[\gamma],\,\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathcal{L}\,\,\vert\,\,\mathcal{L}\in\mathfrak{F}\}.
  \]
  \end{definition}
  Since grupoids are a difficult structure to work with, there is also the easier to work with \emph{holonomy group}, defined at a point $z\in L$ as
  \[
  \mathcal{H}_z(L):=\{[\gamma]\in G(M,\mathfrak{F})\,|\,\gamma(0)=\gamma(1)=z\}.
  \]
  This is naturally a group and can be representen via a surjective homomorphism of the fondamental group of $L$, said surjective homomorphism is called the \emph{holonomy morphism}
  \[
  H:\pi_{1}(L,z)\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_z(L).
  \]
  The prefix on the holonomy groups will be preserved in order to not confuse it with the set of holomorphic functions on a set.


  \section{Bishop's theorems}
  \noindent This sections is as its sayd colloquially, the ``meat and potatos'' of this introductory chapter. Here I present three theorems from Errett Albert Bishop. The breadth of subjects Bishop worked on is wide but there was allways a holomorphic and analytic kernel to it, here in particular I show three theorems that give a bridge between complex algebraic/analytic geometry and complex/functional analyis in Bishop's work.

  \subsection{Bishop's sequence theorem}
  \noindent One of the most consecuental theorems of Bishop shows how the stucture of compact analytic sets are stable under Haussdorff convergence. In the following chapters I will show how this resut can be applied to the theory of holomorphic function on the unit ball of $\con$, the theory of holomorphic foliations on Kähler manifolds and the theory of currents on analytic sets to show similar stability properties on each thory respectively

  \begin{theorem}[Sequence theorem] \label{bishop sequence}
    Let $\lbrace V_n\rbrace$ be a sequence of purely $k$-dimensional subvarieties of a domain $\om\subset\con$ such that $V_n\overset{h}\rightarrow V$, with $V\subset\om$ a closed subset. If
    \[
      \mathrm{Vol}_{2k}(V_n)\leq M \hspace*{0.2cm}\forall n\in\nat,
    \]
    for $M\in\re$ a positive constant, then for the Hausdorff measure we have $\mathcal{H}_{2k+1}(V)=0$, moreover $V$ is a purely $k$-dimensional analytic subvariety of $\om$.
  \end{theorem}

  This theorem is clearly very interesting from various points of views. First it shows how stable are the structure of analytic varieties of pure dimension $k$, a fact that will be useful when studying holomorphic foliations. Another way this theorem is interesting is that it shows that analytic subvarieties with bounded volume do not grow dimensionally and in a way form a closed subset of the Gromov-Hausdorf space.


  \subsection{Bishop's regular coordinates theorem}
  \noindent The next theorem to be exposed in this section is what I call ``Bishop's regular coordinates theorem'', the reason I chose this name will be explained after exposing some of the results that compliment this theorem when its applied to analytic subvarieties of pure dimension $k$ instead of just closed subsets with a certain bound on their Hausdorff dimension.
  \begin{theorem}[Bishop's proper mapping theorem]\label{bishop mapping}
    Let $\om\subset\con$ be a domain that contains $0$ and let $S\subset\om$ be a closed subset. If $\mathcal{H}^{2k+1}(S)=0$, then there is a suitable coordinate change of $\con$, $(z_1,\ldots,z_n)$ and neighborhoods $\om_k\subset\co^k$ and $\om_{n-k}\subset\co^{n-k}$, such that $0\in\om_k\times\om_{n-k}\subset\om$ and the projection
          \[
            \pi_k:S\cap(\om_k\times\om_{n-k})\rightarrow\om_k\, ,\hspace{0.3cm}\pi_k(z,w):=z,
          \]
          \noindent is a proper map.
  \end{theorem}\label{analytic-cover}
  \noindent This theorem applied to purely $k$-dimensional analytic subvarieties of $\con$ implies that the projection $\pi$ is not just a proper mapping, but a finitely sheeted analytic covering outside of a singular set, this is a way to look a desigularization from a geometric point of view, consider the following
  \begin{theorem}
    Let $\om=\om_{k}\times\om_{n-k}\subset\con$ be an open subset and $\pi_{k}:\om\rightarrow\om_{k}$ the cannonical projection onto $\om_{k}$. Let $A\subset\om$ be a purely $k$-dimensional analytic subset such that $\pi=\pi_{k}\vert_{A}:A\rightarrow\om_{k}$ is a proper mapping. Then $A'=\pi(A)\subset\om_{k}$ is an analytic subset and $\pi^{-1}[z_{k}]\cap A$ is locally finite. Moreover in fact, there is an analytic subset $\sig\subset\om_{k}$ of dimension $\dim(\sig)<k$ and a natural number $N\in\nat$ such that
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item $\pi:A\setminus\pi^{-1}(\sig)\to\om_{k}\setminus\sig$ is a locally biholomorphic $N$-sheeted cover
      \item $\pi^{-1}(\sig)$ is nowhere dense in $A$.
    \end{enumerate}
    Any function that satifies the previous two points is called an \emph{analytic cover}.
  \end{theorem}

  This implies that the Hausdorff dimension of an analytic variety is twice its \emph{Krull Dimension} and that the singular part of a vaiety $S=\mathrm{Sing}(A)$ has Hausdorff dimension less than $2\dim(A)-2$, see \cite{Chirka}[pp.38-40]. There is also the connection between proper maps and \emph{regular coordinates of analytic varieties}

  \begin{definition}
  Let $\osheaf_{n}$ be the sheaf of holomorphic functions near $0\in\con$ and let $\psheaf\subset\osheaf$ be a prime ideal, we say that the canonial projections $\pi_{k}(z_{k},z_{n-k})=z_{k}$ are a set of \emph{regular coordinates} if
      \begin{enumerate}
        \item[1] $\mathcal{P}\cap\osheaf_{k}=0$
        \item[2] $\mathcal{F}_{n}=\osheaf_{n}/\mathcal{P}$ is integral over $\mathcal{O}_{k}$
        \item[3] $\mathcal{F}_{n}$ is generated over $\mathcal{F}_{k}=\osheaf_{k}/\mathcal{P}$ by the polynomials $\eta_{k+1}=\pi(z_{k+1})$
      \end{enumerate}
    \end{definition}
    See (\cite{Gunning-Rossi}[pp. 93]). Therefore by the previously discussed theorem, we can prove the existence of a set of reguar coordinates of any purely $k$-dimensional analytic set $A\subset\con$ near $0$ at each of its irreducible components. Lets supose $X\subset\om$ is an \emph{irreducible analytic set of dimension \(k\)}, where $\om=\om_{k}\times\om_{n-k}$ therefore, by Bishop's proper mappping theorem \(\pi=\pi_{k}|_{X}\to\om_{k}\) the usual projection is a \emph{proper mapping}. By theorem \ref{analytic-cover} there  is an anaytic variety $\sigma\subset\om_{k}$ of dimension strictly less than $k$ such that $\pi:X\setminus\pi^{-1}(\sigma)\to\omega\setminus\sigma$ is a covering map and in particular $\pi$ is surjective. Since $X$ is irreducible, its \emph{vanishing ideal} $P=I(X)$ is a \emph{prime ideal}. Now if $f\in\osheaf_{k}\cap P$, then $f(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})=f(z_{1},\dots,z_{k})$ and $f$ vanishes on $X$, i.e. $f$ does not depend on the last $n-k$ variables, we can abuse notation and write this as $f=f\circ\pi_{k}$, now
    \[
      f^{-1}(0)=\pi_{k}^{-1}(f^{-1}(0))\supseteq\pi_{k}^{-1}(\pi(X))=\pi_{k}^{-1}(\om_{k})=\om_{k}\times\om_{n-k}=\om.
    \]
    Therfore $f\equiv0$, or $\osheaf_{k}\cap P=0$, points $2$ and $3$ follow from Weiertrass's division and preparation theorems and the fact that $\pi$ is an \emph{analytic cover}.

  \subsection{Volume growth as a measure for algebraicity}

  \noindent One of the most intersting of Bishop's results that bridges analysis and geometry in a clear way is what I call ``Bishop's polynomial volume growth test for algebraicity''. Following Stolzenberg's presentation (see \cite{Stolzenberg}), the following theorem is a link beween geometrical structures and analysis
  \begin{theorem}[Bishop's volume growth theorem]\label{Bishop1}
          Let $V\subset\con$ be an analytic subvariety of pure complex dimension $k$. If for every $R\in\re^+$
          \[
            \textrm{Vol}_{2k}(V\cap B_R(0))\leq CR^{2k},
          \]
          where $B_R(0)$ denotes the ball centered at the origin with radius $R$, $C\in\re^{+}$ is a positive constant
          and $\textrm{Vol}_{2k}$ is the $2k$-dimensional volume, then $V$ is algebraic.
  \end{theorem}

  I will show how this theorem can be used to prove Chow's theorem in a straightfoward manner. With straightfoward I mean within the theory of analytic sets and their structure without referencing Hilbert's basis theorem or quasi coherent sheafs as other proofs of Chow's thorem utilize these kinds of machinery (see \cite{GAGA},\cite{chow-proofs}). A thing to note about this result is that is very similar to a widely known consequence of Liouville's theorem that states that an analytic function $f$ that grows polynomially i.e. if the norm of the function $|f(z)|$ is $\osheaf(|z|^{k})$ for some $k\in\nat$, then the function is a polynomial. This will be a theme throughout this section, the Bishop's theorems shown here offer similies between analytic functions and analytic varieties. Another similarity to note about this result is with Gromov's theorem on groups of polynomial growth although here the similarities are a bit looser. Using the mprevious theorems the proof is

  \begin{proof} Let $\lbrace R_n\rbrace\subset\re^{+}$ be such that $R_n\rightarrow\infty$ and define $V_n$ as the image of $V\cap B_{R_n}(0)$ by the homothety $z\longmapsto z/R_n$ where $B_R(0)$ is the open ball centered at $0$ as ususal, then $\{V_n\}$ is a sequence of analytic sets of the unit ball with $\textrm{Vol}_{2k}(V_n)<C$, and such that
    \[
      0\in\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty} V_n\text{ in the Hausdorff sense}.
    \]
    Since \(\hsheaf^{2k+1}(V)=0\), by the proper mapping theorem there is a neighborhood of $0$, say $\om=\om_k\times\om_{n-k}$ such that the projection on the first factor $\pi_k$ is a $\sigma$-sheeted branched covering for each $V_n\cap\om$, since $R_n\rightarrow\infty$ the balls $B_{R_n}(0)$ cover the whole of $\co^{n}$ and therefore the projection $\pi_k$ restricted to $V$ is a $\sigma$-sheeted branched covering. From this, is possible to constuct a set of canonically defining functions for $V$ as follows, let
  \[
  \pi_k^{-1}[\pi_k(z)]\cap V=\{\alpha_1(z),\cdots,\alpha_{\sigma}(z)\},
  \]

  \noindent and let $P_{\alpha}:\con\times\con\to\co$ be
  \begin{equation}
  P_{\alpha}(z,w):=\langle z-\alpha_1(z),w\rangle\cdots\langle z-\alpha_{\sigma}(z),w\rangle,
  \end{equation}

  \noindent observe that if $z\in V$, $P_\alpha(z,w)=0$, also for $z$ outside of $V$ we can chose a $w$ fiex such that $\langle z-\alpha_j(z),w\rangle\neq0$ for all $j\in\{1,\dots,\sigma\}$. Now by expanding $P_{\alpha}$ in powers of $w$
  \begin{equation}
  P_{\alpha}(z,w)=\sum_{\vert\mu\vert\leq\sigma} \eta_{\mu}(z)w^{\mu}
  \end{equation}

  \noindent then because $\pi_k$ is a branched covering the functions $\eta_{\mu}$ are analytic and by the previous discussion $V$ is locally the vanishing set of $\eta_{\mu}$ for all $\mu$. Now by applying the same homothety  as before, for a suitable $R_n$, using Cauchy's estimates we have
  \[
  \left\|\dfrac{\partial^{\mu} P_{\alpha}}{\partial^{\mu} w}(z,0)\right\|=\|\eta_{\mu}(z)\|\leq M|\mu|! R_n^{|\mu|}\quad M=\max\{\|P_{\alpha}(z,w)\|\,\vert\,\|z,w\|\leq 1\}
  \]
  therefore by Liouville's theorem the functions $\eta_{\mu}$ are in fact polynomials of degree at most $\sigma-\vert\mu\vert$.
  \end{proof}

  \section{Analytic varieties and Chow's theorem}

  \begin{theorem}[Chow]
    Every closed projective analytic subset is a projective algebraic subset.
  \end{theorem}

  For the proof of this statement, Alberto and I used one consequence of \emph{Bishop's proper mapping theorem} (theorem \ref{bishop mapping}) which was generalized and proved by Bishop in\cite{Bishop}, the result known as Remmert-Stein theorem.

  \begin{theorem}[Remmert-Stein]\label{Rem-Stein}
          Let $\om\subset\con$ be an open subset and $Y$ an analytic subset of $\om$ and let $X$ be a analytic subset of $\om\setminus Y$. If $Y$ is of dimension at most $k-1$ and $X$ is of pure dimension $k$, then the closure of $X$, $\overline{X}\cap\om$ is an analytic subset of $\om$.
  \end{theorem}
  This is an essential step towards the proof of Chow's theorem if one is trying to avoid using categorical methods and
  quasi-coherent sheaves. This is because Remmert and Stein's result imply that the $\textrm{Cone}(X)$ of a projective analytic
  subset of dimension $k\leq n$, $X\subset\cp^n$ is an analytic subset of dimension $k+1$ in $\co^{n+1}$, where the cone is defined as
  \begin{equation}
  \textrm{Cone}(X):=\pi^{-1}[X]\cup\lbrace0\rbrace,\hspace{0.3cm}\pi:\co^{n+1}\setminus\lbrace 0\rbrace\rightarrow\cp^n.
  \end{equation}
  \noindent Here $\pi$ is the usual projection of $\co^{n+1}$ on to the
  projective space, so clearly $\textrm{Cone}(X)=\overline{\pi^{-1}[X]}$, and since $\pi$
  is an analytic projection, $\pi^{-1}[X]$ is an analytic subset. Then from this
  point on, the classical proof is to use the fact that the cone is homothetic-invariant to
  show that the ideal of locally defined holomorphic functions that vanish at the
  cone has a countable basis. Then with Hilbert's basis theorem it is easy to prove the
  fact that the ring of germs of holomorphic functions is Noetherian. This shows
  that $\textrm{Cone}(X)$ is in fact algebraic see for example \cite{Chirka}, but the same result can
  be proved without algebraic methods with an equally simple proof using only
  the geometric and analytical tools presented thus far.  Here is the proof of proof of Chow's theorem via Bishop
  \begin{proof}[Chow via Bishop] By Theorem \ref{Rem-Stein}, $\textrm{Cone}(X):=\overline{\pi^{-1}[X]}$
              is an analytic set of $\co^{n+1}$, and if $X\subset\cp^n$ is of dimension $k$ (real dimension $2k$), then
              $\textrm{Cone}(X)$ has dimension $k+1$. Now the mapping
              $\pi:\sph^{2k+1}\rightarrow\cp^n$ known as \textbf{Hopf's Fibration}, is a \emph{Riemannian fibration} from the sphere with its canonical metric and the \emph{Fubini-Study metric} on projective space, it fibers
              $\cp^n$ into circles (of length $2\pi$), so by Fubini's theorem;
              \[
                  \textrm{Vol}_{2k+1}(\textrm{Cone}(X)\cap\sph^{2n+1})= 2\pi\,\textrm{Vol}^{\cp}_{2k}(X):=A,
              \]
              \noindent where $\textrm{Vol}^{\cp}$ is the ``projective volume'',
              meaning the volume form of the complex projective space given by the Fubini-Study metric.
              Clearly $A$ is finite since $X$ is compact, which means that the volume of the
              intersection $\textrm{Cone}(X)\cap B(R,0)$ grows at most polynomially as
              $R\rightarrow\infty$, since $\textrm{Cone}(X)$ is homothetic-invariant and using
              polar coordinates, we see that
      \[
      \textrm{Vol}_{2k+2}(\textrm{Cone}(X)\cap B(R,0))= \int_{\textrm{Cone}(X)\cap B(R,0)}dr\wedge\sigma_r,
      \]
      \noindent where $\sigma_r$ is the $2k+1$ volume form of
      $S(0,r)\cap{X}$  with
      $S(0,r):=\lbrace\| z\|=r\rbrace\,$ with $0\leq{r}\leq{R}$. We can find a bound for the volume of $\textrm{Cone}(X)\cap B(R,0)$ as follows:

      \[
          \int_{(\textrm{Cone}(X)\cap B(R,0))}dr\wedge\sigma_r=
          \int^R_0 r^{2k+1}dr\,\int_{\textrm{Cone}(X)\cap\sph^{2n+1}}\sigma_1=
          \frac{A}{2k+2}R^{2k+2},
      \]
      \noindent so setting $C=\frac{A}{2k+2}$ by Theorem \ref{bishop} this means
      $\textrm{Cone}(X)$ is algebraic and therefore $X$ is also algebraic.
      \end{proof}

  \subsection{Proof of Montel's Theorem via Bishop}
  We now proceed to prove a generalization of Montel's result of compact subsets of holomorphic functions on the unit disk. We note that there is already another way to prove a version of Montel's result that was proved by Lelong in \cite{Lelong}, although its formulated using the language of integration currents.

  \begin{definition}
    A family $F$ of analytic functions on a domain $\Omega\subset\co^{n}$ is \textbf{normal} in $\Omega$ if every sequence of functions $\lbrace{f_n}\rbrace_{n\in\nat}\subseteq{F}$ contains either a subsequence $\lbrace f_{n_k}\rbrace_{k\in\nat}\subset F$  which converges to a limit function $f\not\equiv\infty$,  uniformly on each compact subset of  $\Omega$, or a subsequence  which converges uniformly to $\infty$ on each compact subset.
  \end{definition}

  The open unit ball on $\con$ will be denoted $B$ with its closure on $\con$ denoted by $\overline{B}$. Let
  \[
    \mathcal{A}(B):=C(\overline{B})\cap\hsheaf(B),
  \]

  \noindent be the Banach algebra of holomorphic functions in $B$ that are
  continuous on $\overline{B}$ and therefore also continuous on $S:=\partial B$. We will prove the following result

  \begin{theorem}[Montel's Theorem] Let $F\subset\mathcal{A}(B)$, be a set of locally bounded functions, i.e.
  for every $z\in B$ there exists an open neighborhood $z\in\Omega\subset B$ such that
  \[
    \| f|_{\Omega}\|_{\infty}=\sup\{|f|\,|\,z\in\Omega\}\leq C_{\Omega},\hspace{0.2cm}\,\text{for some }C\in\re^{+},
  \]

  \noindent then $F$ is a normal set.
  \end{theorem}
  We are going to prove this by means of Bishop's results. First, we note that the graph of a holomorphic function $f:B\rightarrow\co$ is an analytic subset of pure dimension $n$ on $B\times\co$ since $\Gamma_{f}=V_B(w-f(z))=\{(z,w):w-f(z)=0), z\in B, w\in\co\}$ (\ref{vanishing-locus}), where $\Gamma_{f}$ denotes the graph of $f$, and $z=(z_1,\dots,z_n)\in\con$.
  Also if $\{f|_{\Omega}\}$ are bounded for all $f\in F$, then $\textrm{Vol}_{2n}(\Gamma_{f|_{\Omega}})$ have uniformly bounded volume as we shall see.
  \begin{proof}
  Let $\epsilon\in(0,1)$ and we take
  $B_{\epsilon}=\overline{B(1-\epsilon,0)}\subset B$, since
  $B_{\epsilon}$ is a compact subset of $B$, we have that $F$ is uniformly
  bounded in $B_{\epsilon}$. Let $C\in\re^{+}$ be a bound for $|
  f(z)|$ for all $z\in B_{\epsilon}$ and $f\in F$, then by Cauchy's
  integral formula for the open ball (see \cite{rudin}[Ch. 3, 3.2.4.]), if
  $S_{\epsilon}:=\partial B_{\epsilon}$, then
  \begin{equation}
    f(z)=\int_{S_{\epsilon}} \frac{f(\zeta)}{(1-\langle z,\zeta\rangle)^{n}}\,d\sigma(\zeta)
    \hspace{0.3cm}\,\forall z\in B_{\epsilon}\setminus S_{\epsilon},
  \end{equation}

  \noindent where $\sigma$ is the usual Lebesgue measure in $S_{\epsilon}$. Taking the derivative  with
  respect to $z_{j}$ we obtain

  \begin{equation}
    \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial z_j}(z)=\int_{S_{\epsilon}} \frac{f(\zeta)n\zeta_j}{(1-\langle z,\zeta\rangle)^{n+1}}\,d\sigma(\zeta)
    \hspace{0.3cm}\,\forall z\in B_{\epsilon}\setminus S_{\epsilon},
  \end{equation}
  \noindent therefore, we have the following bound for the partial derivatives of
  all $f\in F$ and $z\in B_{\epsilon}\setminus S_{\epsilon}$,
  \begin{equation}\label{bound derivative}
          \left|\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial z_j}(z)\right|\leq C \frac{n \textrm{Vol}_{2n-1}(S_{\epsilon})(1-\epsilon)}{(1-(1-\epsilon)^2)^{n+1}}.
  \end{equation}
  Now, to show that the hypotheses of theorem \ref{bishop sequence} are satisfied, we use
  this constant bound of the derivatives to show that for a sequence of graphs of
  functions $\lbrace f_n\rbrace_{n\in\nat}$, the $2n$ dimensional volumes of
  their graphs are uniformly bounded in $B_{\epsilon}$ by a constant
  $M\in\re^{+}$. This is because the volume of $\Gamma_f$ is given by
  \begin{equation}\label{graph volume}
          \textrm{Vol}_{2n}(\Gamma_{f})=\int_{B_{\epsilon}}|\lambda(z)|\,dx_1\ldots dx_{n}dy_1\ldots dy_n,
  \end{equation}
  \noindent where $z_j=x_j+iy_j$ and $\lambda:B_{\epsilon}\rightarrow\re$ is given by the pullback of the
  parametrization function $\varphi(z)=(z,f(z))$
  \[
    \varphi^*\omega=\lambda(z)(dx_1\wedge dy_1)\wedge\ldots\wedge(dx_i\wedge dy_n),
  \]
  \noindent with $\omega$ being the volume form of $\Gamma_f\subset B\times\co$. A straightforward calculation
  shows:
  \begin{equation}
          \lambda(z)=\sqrt{1 + \|\nabla f\|^2},
  \end{equation}
  \noindent with
  \[
    \nabla f =\Big(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial z_1},\dots,\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial z_n}\Big).
  \]
  Therefore, the volumes of all the graphs are uniformly bounded by (\ref{bound derivative}) and (\ref{graph volume}). Now the only thing left to show is the
  convergence with respect to the Hausdorff metric of a subsequence of
  $\lbrace\Gamma_{f_n}\rbrace$ in $B_{\epsilon}$. This follows from
  the fact that $| f(z)|$ is uniformly bounded in $B_{\epsilon}$ for all
  $f\in F$. Since the image of all $f_n$ are inside a compact set, it therefore follows that for
  $z\in S_{\epsilon}$ there exists a convergent subsequence $\lbrace
  f_{n_k}(z)\rbrace$ and by Cauchy's integral formula $\lbrace f_{n_k}(z)\rbrace$ converges uniformly on $B_{\epsilon}\setminus
  S_{\epsilon}$. This means that $\Gamma_{f_{n_k}}\rightarrow\Gamma_f$ in the Hausdorff metric with
  $\Gamma_f\subset B_{\epsilon}\times\co$, and by theorem \ref{bishop sequence}
  we know $\Gamma_f|_{B_{\epsilon}}$ is an analytic subset of pure complex
  dimension $n$. Moreover, it is clearly the graph of a holomorphic function
  \hbox{$f:B_{\epsilon}\setminus S_{\epsilon}\rightarrow\co$} since at each fiber
  $\lbrace z_0\rbrace\times\co$, the intersection of each graph
  $\Gamma_{f_{n_k}}$ gives a unique point $(z_0,f_{n_k}(z_0))$.
  If there were two distinct points $(z_0,w_1)$ and $(z_0,w_2)$ at the
  intersection $(\lbrace z_0\rbrace\times\co)\cap\Gamma_f$, then taking a
  compact set $K$ containing $\lbrace f_{n_k}(z_0)\rbrace\cup\lbrace
  w_1,w_2\rbrace$, by Hausdorff convergence we would have that the limit
  of $\lbrace f_{n_k}(z_0)\rbrace$ is not unique. Thus
  $\Gamma_f$ has to be the graph of a holomorphic function in $B_{\epsilon}$ for all
  $\epsilon\in(0,1)$. By analytic continuation, we thus define $f$ in all of $B$. The graph
  of $\Gamma_f$ can be defined by
  $\Gamma_f=\left\{(z,w)\,:\,\,F(z,w):=f(z)-w=0,\, z\in{B}, w\in\,\mathbb{C}\right\}$. The graph
  is the zero locus of the holomorphic function $F(z,w)=f(z)-w$, since
  $\partial{F}/\partial{w}=1$, the complex gradient of $F$ does not vanish; it follows by the implicit function theorem that $\Gamma_f$ is smooth.
  \end{proof}

  \section{Foliations on Kähler manifolds with all leaves compact}
  \noindent In this final section, we apply Bishop's sequence theorem (theorem \ref{bishop sequence})
  to prove a version of \cite{EMS}[Theorem 1], for the particular case of compact complex foliations on
  Kähler manifolds with all leaves compact, also we prove that a holomorphic foliation in a compact Kähler manifold with at least \textbf{one} compact leaf with finite holonomy, then all of its leaves are compact.

  \begin{theorem}\label{kahlerEMS}
  Let $M$ be a compact connected Kähler manifold
  of complex dimension $n$ and $\mathfrak{F}$ a holomorphic foliation
  with leaves of complex dimension $d<n$ and with all leaves compact,
  then:
  \begin{enumerate}
          \item[1] The $2d$-dimensional volume (with
                  respect to the Kähler metric) of the leaves is uniformly
                  bounded.
          \item[2] The quotient space $M/\mathfrak{F}$ is a complex orbifold and
          the quotient map $\pi:M\to{M/\mathfrak{F}}$ is an analytic map.

          \item[3] The singular set $\mathcal S$ of $M/\mathfrak{F}$ is the saturated set that corresponds to the leaves with nontrivial holonomy group (which, by the first proposition, is a finite group). Thus, the singular set $S$ is an analytic subset of $M/\mathfrak{F}$. In particular, since $M$ is connected ($\mathcal S$ is of real codimension greater than one), it follows that the complement ${\mathcal R}:=M\setminus{\mathcal S}$ is connected and the  $2d$-dimensional volume (with
  respect to the Kähler metric) of leaves in $\mathcal R$ is constant.
  \end{enumerate}
  \end{theorem}

  First, throughout this theorem and the following results, we are going to make use of the following fundamental theorem, due to W. Thurston (\cite{Thurston}) and called the {\it generalized Reeb stability theorem}:
  \begin{theorem}[W. Thurston \cite{Thurston}]\label{Reeb}
  Let $\fsheaf$ be a smooth foliation of dimension $m$ on the smooth, compact $n$-manifold $M$
  ($0\leq{m}\leq{n}$) and $\mathcal{L}$ be a compact leaf without holonomy. Then, there exists a \emph{saturated} neighborhood, say $\Omega$, of $\mathcal{L}$ such that $\Omega$ is diffeomorphic to a product $\mathcal{L}\times D^{n-m}$ were
  $D\subset{\mathbb R}^{n-m}$ is an open disk, such that every leaf in $\Omega$ is of the form $\mathcal{L}\times{\left\{p\right\}}$ with $p\in{D}$. Moreover, if $\mathcal{L}$ has \textbf{\emph{finite holonomy}}, there exists a \emph{saturated} tubular neighborhood of
  $\mathcal{L}$, $\Omega$ in which the foliation is a flat disk bundle over $\mathcal{L}$.
  In particular, if $M$ is a compact complex manifold (not necessarily Kähler) and $\fsheaf$ is a holomorphic foliation, there exists a saturated neighborhood, $\Omega$ of $\mathcal{L}$ such that $\Omega$ is diffeomorphic (although not necessarily biholomorphic) to a product
    $\mathcal{L}\times D$ with $D\subset\co^{q}$ an open polydisk, such that every leaf in $\Omega$ is of the form $\mathcal{L}\times{\left\{p\right\}}$ with $p\in{D}$
  \end{theorem}
  The proof of the second proposition of the theorem uses the following
  {\it Quotient Theorem} of H. Cartan:

  \end{theorem}

We finish this paper with a proof of a result of Jorge Vitorio Pereira, which guarantees the compactness of all leaves in a compact Kähler manifold provided that it is known that at least \textbf{one} of the leaves is compact and has finite holonomy (see \cite{Pereira}[Theorem 1]).
\begin{theorem}\label{compact-leaf}
Let $M$ be a compact connected Kähler manifold of dimension $n$ and $\mathfrak{F}$ be a holomorphic foliation of codimension $q<n$ for which there is at least one compact leaf with finite holonomy, then all leaves are compact with finite holonomy or equivalently their volumes are uniformly bounded and the leaf space is Hausdorff.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  Let us denote by $\mathcal L_{0}$ to the compact leaf with finite holonomy of $\mathfrak F$ and define the following set
\[
    \mathcal{U}:=\{x\in M\,|\, \text{ the leaf through $x$ is compact with finite holonomy}\}.
\]
Clearly $\mathcal{L}\subset\Omega\subset\mathcal{U}$ and therefore $\mathcal{U}$ is a non-empty saturated set.
Also by the same argument as before every leaf in $\mathcal{U}$ has a tubular saturated neighborhood comprised of
compact leaves with finite holonomy, therefore for every point $z\in\mathcal{U}$ there is an open saturated
neighborhood $W$ of $z$ such that $z\in W\subset\mathcal{U}$ i.e. \(\mathcal{U}\) is an \emph{open} saturated set.
Note that $\mathcal{U}$ is the union of all saturated open sets with all its leaves compact and with finite holonomy;
another way to describe it is as the maximal open set of saturated sets with all its leaves compact and with finite holonomy.
We show that this set is also closed to finish the proof. Let $\{z_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{U}$ be a convergent sequence
with limit $z_{n}\rightarrow z_{0}$, since $\{z_{n}\}$ is convergent, in particular is a Cauchy sequence.
Moreover, let $\mathcal{L}_{n}$ be the leaf through $z_{n}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{0}$
the leaf through $z_{0}$, since $z_k\in\mathcal{U}$, every $\mathcal{L}_k$ is compact with finite holonomy, to show
$z_{0}\in\mathcal{U}$ we need to show $\mathcal{L}_{0}$ is compact and has finite holonomy, first since $\{z_{k}\}$ is a Cauchy
sequence, the leaves $\{\mathcal{L}_{k}\}$ are eventually arbitrarily close between them. Since all of them are compact
submanifolds of a Kähler manifold, they minimize the volume of their homology classes respectively, therefore, there is a
$N\in\nat$ such that for all \(\{\mathcal{L}_{k}\,|\,k\geq N\}\) are in the same homological class, therefore eventually the
volume of $\{\mathcal{L}_k\}$ is constant.
Now for any foliated chart $V$ around $z_{0}$, let $\mathcal{L}_{k}\cap V$ be a sequence in the intersection
$V\cap\mathcal{U}$, since $z_{0}$ is a limit point of $\mathcal{U}$ and $\mathcal{U}\cap V\neq\emptyset$, by convergence
there is a $N\in\nat$ such that for $k\geq N$, $z_k\in V$. Moreover, since $V$ is a foliated chart,
$V$ is biholomorphic to a product $W_q\times W_{n-q}$ and every leaf $\{\mathcal{L}_k\cap V\}_{k\geq N}$ is the set
$\varphi^{-1}(W_q\times\{z_k\})$, here we abused the notation a little bit by identifying the points of the
convergent sequence with their corresponding transversal coordinates.
Its clear that \hbox{$V\cap\mathcal{L}_{k}\rightarrow V\cap\mathcal{L}_{0}$} as closed subsets of $\mathcal{U}\cap V$,
since $z_k\rightarrow z_0$ and the plaques $W_q\times\{z_k\}$ clearly converge to $W_q\times\{0\}$ by continuity of $\varphi$.
Therefore, since \(\mathcal{L}_k\) are compact connected, and with finite (linear) holonomy and bounded volume,
by Bishop's sequence theorem (theorem \ref{bishop sequence}), \(\{\mathcal{L}_k\}\) converges to a closed (compact) pure-dimensional analytic subset of $\mathcal{L}_{0}$ that is tangent to $\mathfrak{F}$ at $z_0$ therefore
\hbox{$\mathcal{L}_{k}\rightarrow\mathcal{L}_{0}$} since leaves are connected by definition.
This means that $\mathcal{L}_{0}$ is compact analytic set with finite holonomy since the volume of the leaves is bounded and nearby leaves cover $\mathcal{L}_{0}$,
therefore $\mathcal{U}=M$ since its both open and closed and $M$ is connected.
\end{proof}

\subsubsection*{Beauville conjecture}
However for compact Kähler manifolds if \emph{just one} leaf is compact then all are, moreover the holonomy of the foliation is finite and therefore all the leaves have the same \emph{universal cover}. Therefoer the problem I tried to solve with the techniques of this thesis was the problem of finding the universal covering space of a foliated compact Kähler manifold with all leaves compact. This problem is within the setting of compact Kähler manifolds for which their tangent bundle splits, this is what Beauville's conjecture is about (see \cite{Beauville}).
\begin{theorem}[Beauville's conjecture (2002)]\label{Beauville}
        Let $X$ be a compact Kähler variety such that there exists a holomorphic decomposition of its tangent bundle
        \[
        TM=\bigoplus_{i\in I}\mathcal{F}_i\hspace{0.2cm}\text{such that each}\hspace{0.2cm}\bigoplus_{i\in J}\mathcal{F}_i\,,\,J\subset I\hspace{0.2cm}\text{is involutive},
        \]
        then the universal cover of $M$ is isomorphic to a product $\widetilde{M}\cong\prod_{i\in I}U_i$ such that induces an isomorphism
        \[
          T\widetilde{M}\cong\bigoplus_{i\in I}\widetilde{\mathcal{F}_i}
        \]
      where $\widetilde{\mathcal{F}_{i}}$ projects onto $\mathcal{F}_{i}$ under the covering map.
\end{theorem}
Recently, there has been some progress on this problem done by Druel, Pereira, Pym and Touzet, whom proved a version of this conjecture in the context of manifolds with aditional \emph{Poisson structure}, but with the particular focus on Poisson, see \cite{DPPT}. Here is the statement that they proved

\begin{theorem}[Druel, Pereira, Pym and Touzet (2022)]\label{DPPT}
Suppose that $X$ is a compact Kähler manifold equipped with a splitting $TX=\mathfrak{F}\oplus\mathfrak{G}$ of the tangent bundle into involutive subbundles. If F has a compact leaf $\mathcal{L}$ with finite holonomy group, then the splitting of $TX$ is induced by a splitting of the universal cover of $X$ as a product of manifolds. If, in addition,
$L$ has finite fundamental group and trivial canonical class, then the splitting of the universal cover is induced by a splitting of a finite étale cover. 
\end{theorem}

Its clear that this a stronger statement, than theorem \ref{compact-leaf}, so it is a natural to see if the proof they gave has something to do with volumes of the leaves or it can be proved in that way. The proof makes use of Reeb stability, however then they define a flat Ehresmann connection for the \emph{holonomy grupoid fibration} $s:\mathrm{Hol}(\mathfrak{F})\to X$ whose monodromy representation induces the holonomy representation of every leaf. The difficulty here for trying to prove this with Hausdorff or even Gromov-Hausdorff convergence of the leaves has been stracting topological information of the universal cover, it will be interesting to see if is possible to do so.

\noindent\emph{\bf Idea:} Under these assumptions, I believe it may be possible to define a biholomorphism between the universal covers of the leaves by means of liftings of curves and work with the holonomy groupoid of the foliation similar to what was done in \cite{DPPT}. That is, in the limit of a sequence of leaves with bounded volume, their universal cover is the same and the fundamental group of the limit has as a subgroup that of the leaves approaching it, it may be necessary to ask that these groups be finite. Essentially, I believe that if a leaf of the foliation is approximated by others, the topology should not grow, and this reflects that there are vanishing "loops" cycles, meaning that generators of the fundamental group should disappear in the limit as they become small. \subsubsection*{Poisson Varieties, Weinstein's Theorem and its Natural Foliation}
\noindent Here is a short summary on the structure of \emph{Kähler-Poisson varieties} and after, there will be a short discussion on if the techniques developed in this thesis are applicable if at all to the problmes described, I was inspired by the proof of Theorem \ref{DPPT} and I believe that there may exist interesting relationships between the volume function of the leaves defined by the natural foliation of a \emph{Poisson} variety (Theorem \ref{weins}) and the structure of a Fano-Poisson variety.

A complex manifold or variety $X$ is called a Poisson if there exists a bilinear operation on the ring of germs of holomorphic functions on $X$, known as \emph{Poisson bracket},
\begin{equation}
    \{\cdot,\cdot\}:\osheaf_{X}\times\osheaf_{X}\rightarrow\osheaf_{X},
\end{equation}
  that is the analogue of the lie bracket for functions and as such, satisfies the following properties
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Anticommutative: $\{f,g\} = -\{g,f\}$
    \item Leibnitz's rule: $\{f,gh\}=\{f,g\}h + g\{f,h\}$
    \item Jacobi identity: $\{f,\{g,h\}\}+\{g,\{h,f\}\} + \{h,\{ f,g\}\}=0$.
\end{enumerate}

Note that a germ of a fixed function $H\in\osheaf_{X}$ definition a vector field given by $\xi_{X}(\cdot)=\{H,\cdot\}$, this vector field is called the \emph{Hamiltonian} field defined by $H$. Many questions about integrability of foliations are posed in terms of their Hamiltonians, and have their root comming from physics, this aproach was never the main viwepoitn of this thesis but it is important and interesting to point it out as many research areas today are related to thier applications to physics and Poisson manifolds/varieties are one of the main ones.

Using the notation of \emph{multivector} fields, an alternative definition of the Poisson bracket is the following: denoting the holomorphic vector fields as $\mathcal{T}X=H^0(X,TX)$,
then the space of \emph{$p$-vectors} is defined by
\[
    \Lambda^{p}(\mathcal{T}X):=\{\osheaf_X\times\dots\times\osheaf_X\rightarrow\osheaf_X\,\vert\,\text{anti-symmetric}\}.
\]
Then, a Poisson bracket is a \emph{bivector} field, which we can definitionne via the pairing $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$
between the $p$-vector fields and the space of \emph{holomorphic $p$-differential forms} $\om^{p}(X)$ by
\[
        \pi\in H^0(X,\Lambda^2(\mathcal{T}X))\,\text{ then }\,\{f,g\}=\langle \pi,df\wedge dg\rangle.
\]
Therefore, generalizing this we have the following map defined by a Poisson bracket $\pi$
\[
        \pi^{\#}:\om^1_X\rightarrow\mathcal{T}X,\hspace{0.2cm}\pi^{\#}(\alpha):=\iota_{\alpha}(\pi):=\langle\pi,\alpha\wedge\cdot\rangle.
\]
\noindent With this, the rank of $\pi$ at a point $p\in X$ is defined as the positive integer $r\in \zah^{+}$ that defines the dimension of the maximum space where $\pi^{\#}_p$ is non-degenerate, i.e., the dimension of the largest space such that $\pi^{\#}$ is a bijection. If $\pi^{\#}$ is non-degenerate, its rank is $2n=\dim(X)$, then $\pi^{-1}$, the inverse of $\pi^{\#}$, defines a symplectic form on $X$. Weinstein's splitting theorem implies that ther is a natural foliation on a Poisson variety $(X,\pi)$.

First, a definition of the type of morphism that will be considered for the theory; a holomorphic map between two Poisson varieties $(X_1,\{\cdot,\cdot\}_1)$ and $(X_2,\{\cdot,\cdot\}_2)$ is a \emph{Poisson morphism} $\phi:X_1\to X_2$ if $\{f,g\}_1\circ\phi=\{f\circ\phi,g\circ\phi\}_2$.
\begin{theorem}[Darboux-Weinstein]\label{weins}
        Let $(X,\pi)$ be a holomorphic Poisson manifold of real dimension $2n$. Suppose that $\pi$ has rank $2r$ at a point $x\in X$,
        then there exists a neighborhood $U$ of $x$ such that $U$ is isomorphic in the Poisson sense to a product $S\times P$, where $S$ is symplectic with coordinates $(p_i,q_i)_{i=1}^r$, and $(P,\tilde{\pi})$ is a Poisson manifold of rank zero at $x$ with coordinates $z=(z_j)_{j=1}^{2n-2r}$, o theses coordinates the Poisson bracket is written as
        \[
                \pi=\sum_{i=1}^r \partial{p_i}\wedge\partial{q_i}+\sum_{1\leq j\leq k\leq 2n-2r} f^{jk}(z)\partial{z_j}\wedge\partial{z_k}.
        \]
\end{theorem}
\noindent This theorem clearly defines a natural foliation on $(M,\pi)$ by symplectic leaves, however, not all leaves have the same dimension, so it is necessary to note that the definition of foliation can be expanded to this more general context, i.e., a foliation is simply an involutive $\osheaf_X$-submodule of $\mathcal{T}X$. This defines a filtration $X_0\subset X_2\subset X_4\subset\dots\subset M$, where \hbox{$X_{2k}=\{x\in X\,|\,\textrm{rank}(\pi_x)\leq 2k\}$},
if we denote the symplectic leaves of $(X,\pi)$ as $\mathcal{L}$, then we can also think of $X_{2k}$ as
\[
X_{2k}=\bigcup_{\dim(\mathcal{L})\leq 2k}\mathcal{L}.
\]

\noindent\emph{\bf Idea:} Study the growth of the volume of the sets $X_{2k}\cap B_{R}(x)$ where $B_R(x)$ is the metric ball of radius $R$ centered at a point $x\in X_{2k}$ when $R\rightarrow\infty$. If the set $X_{2k}$ is of dimension greater than $2k$, we expect a volume growth behavior greater than $\osheaf(R^{2k}).$

\subsubsection*{Gromov-Hausdorff limits on Fano varieties}
The work of Donaldson Chen and Sun reveal important information about the geometric structure of Fano manifolds, in recent years they have developed the theory that explains the realtion ship between the stability of Fano manofolds and their metrics  
\begin{theorem}[Chen-Donaldson-Sun, 2015; Tian, 2015]
  A smooth Fano manifold $X$ is K-polystable if and only if it admits a Kähle-Einstein metric.
\end{theorem}
Where the stability mentioned here, \emph{K-stability}; is a type of stability for complex algebraic varieties and is part of  what is known as \emph{\bf Geometric Invariant Theory}. Originaly introduced by Gang Tian and further developed by Donaldson K-stability is defined for a \emph{polarised variety} $(X,L)$, where $X$ is a complex variety and $L$ an ample line bundle on $X$, as such all Fano varieties are polarized. Now K-stability uses a \emph{test configuration} which consist on the pair $(\mathcal{X}, K^{*}_{\mathcal{X}})$ for $(X, K^{*}_X)$ shuch that
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item $\mathcal{X}$ is a scheme with a $\co^{*}$ action 
    \item a $\co^*$-equivariant ample line bundle $\lsheaf$ on $\mathcal{X}$,
    \item and a flat $\co^{*}$ equivariant map (acting by multiplication) $\pi:\mathcal{X}\to\co$, where the fibre $X_1= \pi^{-1}(1)$ and $L_1=\lsheaf|_{X_1}$ are isomerphic as a line bundle to $(X,L^r)$ for some $r\in\nat$.
  \end{enumerate}
  The \emph{Donaldson-Futaki invariant} $\mathbf{{DF}}(\mathcal{X}, \lsheaf)$ is a rational number that measures the weight of the $\co^*$-action on the central fibre. For a normal test configuration over $\cp^1$ it is given by the intersection-theoretic formula
\[
  \mathbf{DF}(\mathcal{X}, \lsheaf) \;=\; \frac{n}{n+1}\,
  \frac{\overline{\lsheaf}^{\,n+1}}{(K^{*}_X)^n}
  \;+\; \frac{\overline{K_{\mathcal{X}/\cp^1}} \cdot \overline{\lsheaf}^{\,n}}
             {(K^{*}_X)^n},
\]
where $\overline{\lsheaf}$ and $\overline{K_{\mathcal{X}/\cp^1}}$ are the natural extensions to the compactification $\overline{\mathcal{X}} \to \cp^1$.
 
\begin{definition}
  A Fano variety $X$ is:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{K-semistable} if $\mathbf{DF}(\mathcal{X},\lsheaf) \geq 0$ for every
      normal test configuration $(\mathcal{X},\lsheaf)$.
    \item \textbf{K-stable} if $\mathbf{DF}(\mathcal{X},\lsheaf) > 0$ for every
      non-trivial normal test configuration.
    \item \textbf{K-polystable} if $\mathbf{DF}(\mathcal{X},\lsheaf) \geq 0$ and equality
      holds precisely when $(\mathcal{X},\lsheaf)$ is a product configuration
      (outside codimension 2).
    \item \textbf{Uniformly K-stable} if there exists $\varepsilon > 0$
      such that $\mathbf{DF}(\mathcal{X},\lsheaf) \geq \varepsilon \lVert (\mathcal{X},\lsheaf)
      \rVert_{\min}$ for all test configurations $(\mathcal{X},\lsheaf)$.
  \end{itemize}
\end{definition}
This are the implications for this stabilities: Uniformly K-stable $\Rightarrow$ K-stable $\Rightarrow$ K-semistable.

Now, in terms of volumes, limits and extensions of Kähler manifols, Donaldson and Song Sun in their two papers ``Gromov-Hausdorff Limits of Kähler Manifolds and Algebraic Geometry'' and the sequel with the same name but with a II, they stablish techniques for studying the Gromov-Hausdorff limits of Kähler manifolds, they prove the existence of Kähler-Einstein metrics given certain curvature and algebraic information. More precisely, they consider a \emph{compact complex hermitian manifold}, i.e. data of the type $(X,g,J,L,A)$ where $(X,g)$ is a compact Riemannian manifold of \emph{\bf even} dimension, with a complex structure $J$, such that $(X,g,J)$ is a Kähler manifold i.e. $\omega(\cdot,\cdot)=g(J(\cdot),\cdot)$ is symplectic and $L$ is a Hermitian line bundle over $X$ with a connection $A$ and curvature $-i\omega$, such data will be simply denoted as $X$ for brebity. Moreover the following applies
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The metric satisfies the following upper and lower bounds for the \emph{Ricci curvature tensor}
        \begin{equation}
          -\frac{g}{2}\leq\mathrm{Ric}\leq g
        \end{equation}
  \item $X\in\mathcal{K}(n,c,V)$ where $V$,$c$ are positive reals and $\mathcal{K}(n,c,V)$ denotes the class of all manifolds such that $\mathrm{Vol}(X)=V$, $n=\dim(X)$ that satify the ``non-collapsing condition'' i.e. for any metric ball of radious $R$ of $X$ the following inequality holds
        \begin{equation}
          \mathrm{Vol}(B_R)\geq c\frac{\pi^n}{n!}R^{2n}
        \end{equation}
  \item The connection  induces a holomorphic structure on and for each $k\in\nat$ there is a natural $\lsheaf^2$ hermitian metric on the space $H^{0}(X,L^k)$ with Bergman function $\rho_k,X$ defined by any orthonormal basis of $\{s_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\Lambda}\subset H^{0}(X,L^k)$ as
        \begin{equation}
          \rho_{k,X}=\sum_{\alpha\in\Lambda}\|s_{\alpha}\|^2
          \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
Note that for such manifolds $X$ they are all \emph{polarized} manifolds. Then they prove that the class of manifolds in $\mathcal{K}(n,c,V)$ are all embedabble by sections of $L^k$ for a fixed $k$ as \emph{projective algebraic manifolds} in some $\cp^N$ and that their Gromov-Hausdorff limits converge to a normal projective variety of $\cp^N$. The proof is beautifull, long and intricate, it relies on the so called ``Hormander technique'' and of course Gromov-Hausdorff converge developed by Anderson, Bishop, Cheeger, Colding, Gromoll and Gromov among others. Then in a very similar manner to Bishop, they (specially in the sequen paper) relate the algebreo-geometric structure of the Gromov-Hausdorff limits to its curvature, see \cite{D-SS} and \cite{D-SSII}.

\chapter{Currents and Lelong numbers}
\noindent From now, the focus will be on \emph{\bf integration currents} which are dual objects to \emph{differential forms} via integration, these objects encode geometric information such as volume of subvarieties and topologial/homological information as they encode the dual homological theory to DeRham cohomology, moreover its possible to calculate an equivalent  to intersection numbers, known as \emph{Lelong numbers}. Lelong showed that the volumes of analytic subsets and the \emph{\bf mass} of their integration currents are directly related to the analytic structure in the same way Bishop showed it for volumes, futhermore the notion of Lelong numbers and the Lelong sublevel sets will be very useful for some applications to foliations. Quoting from Lelong's 1969 article, \emph{Propiétés métriques des ensembles analytiques complexes}  
\begin{quote}
The notion of "area" of complex analytic sets is now clearly established. It derives from the existence of the integral of a differential form over such a set. This definition is the basis of a method for studying the metric properties of complex analytic sets. In turn, in the study of these, a particular class of linear operators appears: currents or generalized differential forms, which are at once positive and closed. In more abstract terms, one seeks a functor from the category of complex analytic sets to the category of such currents. For the search of this functor, it seems that a new and effective viewpoint is provided by the theorems of the extension of analytic sets such as the Remmert-Stein theorem and the proof of W. Stoll's theorem, proved by E. Bishop. On the other hand, a closed family of analytic sets is a normal family, which is compared with P. Montel's classical theorem for holomorphic functions.
\end{quote}

\noindent Here I will expose some of the results form Lelong and also general results of currents that will be useful in the next chapter about the generalization of the previous results to the singular setting. 

Let $X$ be a pure dimension $k$ analytic subset defined in a domain $U\subset\mathbb{C}^n$, and $\om^{(k,k)}_c(U)$ the space of differential forms with $C^{\infty}$ coefficients of compact support of type $(k,k)$, then integration on $X$ defines the following linear operator $\tau:\om^{(k,k)}_c(U)\to\co$

\begin{equation}\label{integration-current}
    \tau(\phi)=\int_{X}\phi,\quad\phi\in\om^{(k,k)}_c(U).
\end{equation}

Now, given an analytic set $X$, the analytic set of regular points of $X$ is $X_{reg}:=X\setminus \sig(X)\subset U':=U\setminus \sig(X)$. The set $U'$ is said to be the open subset of regular points of $U$, then the problem of finding $\tau(\phi)$ is the problem of extending the operator
\[
    \tau_0(\phi)=\int_{X_{reg}}\phi
\]
from the space $\om^{(k,k)}_c(U')$ (where it is well-defined in the classical sense) to $\om^{(k,k)}_c(U)$. This is a way to reformulate in terms of \emph{integration currents} the problem that Bishop tackled and solved. More generally, the space of currents is the dual space of differential forms which is why they are also know as \emph{generalized differential forms} or differential form with coefficients that are generalized functions. Here the term ``generalized function'' refers to what is more formally refered by the annoingly overused term ``distributions'', which are the continuos linear functionals of $\csheaf^{\infty}(U)$ for any open set $U\subset\re^n$. More formally, for any $U\subset\re^n$ open set and $Y$ a Banach space, lets denote the vector space of infinitely differentiable functions as $\csheaf^{\infty}(U,Y)$, now, this space is endorsed with a locally convex, translation invariant Hausdorff topology defined by the family of seminorms $\{\rho^{m}_K\}_{m\in\nat}$, where $K\subset U$ is a compact subset and $\rho$ is defined by
\[
  \rho^{m}(\phi)_K=\sup\{\|\mathrm{D}^j\phi\|\,|\,j\in\{0,\dots,m\},\,x\in K\}.
\]
Where $\|\mathrm{D}^j\phi(x)\|$ is the punctual norm of $j$'th derivative of $\phi$ as a \emph{linear operator} at $x\in U$. Now the space $\csheaf^{\infty}_c(U,Y)$ of differentiable functions with \emph{compact support} on $U$ with values in $Y$ has same topology, and by local convexity it has enough linear functionals,
\begin{definition}\label{distribution}
  Let $U\subset\re^n$ and $Y$ be a Banach space, the {\bf space of distributions of type Y} is defined as the space of continuous linear functionals
  \begin{equation}
    \dsheaf(U,Y):=\csheaf^{\infty}_c(U,Y)^{*}=\{T:\csheaf^{\infty}_c(U,Y)\to\co\,|\,T\text{ is linear and continuous}\},
  \end{equation}
  where $\dsheaf(U,Y)$ is endowed with the {\bf weak topology}.  
\end{definition}
Now, the space of currents will be a space of distributions of certain type $Y$ and of these so called currents, the interesting ones for the purposes of this work will be the currents than can be written as an integration as in \ref{integration-current}. There is lot of work about this topic for distribution in general see \cite{federer}, in our case we will work in the spaces
\begin{equation}
\dsheaf^{k}(U):=\dsheaf(U,\bigwedge^k\re^n)\quad\text{and}\quad\dsheaf_{\co}^{k}(U):=\dsheaf(U,\bigwedge^k\re^n)\otimes_{\re}\co
\end{equation}
for real al complex valued currents respectively, these are called the spaces of $k$ {\bf\emph{dimensional currents}}. For complex currents there is the usual decompostion into what are called $(p,q)$-currents analogous to the decompostion of $(p,q)$-forms
\[
  \dsheaf_{\co}^{k}(U)=\dsheaf(U,\bigwedge^k\re^n)\otimes_{\re}\co=\bigoplus_{p+q=k}\dsheaf^{(p,q)}(U)
\]
Where the space of currents of bidegree $(p,q)$ on $U$ are denoted $\dsheaf^{(p,q)}(U)$, usually we will work with currents on $\dsheaf^{(k,k)}(U)$
The space of currents can be described as the space of expressions of the type
\begin{equation}
    \tau=\sum_{i_1,\dots i_{n-k},j_1,\dots j_{n-k}}\tau_{i_1\dots i_{n-k}j_1\dots j_{n-k}} dz_{i_1}\wedge\dots\wedge dz_{i_{n-k}}\wedge d\overline{z}_{j_1}\wedge\dots\wedge d\overline{z}_{j_{n-k}}.
\end{equation}
Usually these espressions can be shortened by setting $I=\{i_1,\dots,i_{n-k}\}$ and $J=\{j_1,\dots,j_{n-k}\}$, each $\tau_{I,J}$ is a linear functional on the space of $\csheaf^{\infty}(U)$ in this thesis the name ``current'' or ``integration current'' will mostly be used. Moreover the space of currents can be thought of as the dual space of smooth differential forms of bidegree $(n-p,n-q)$ with compact support, meaning $\dsheaf^{(p,q)}(U)=\om_c^{(p,q)}(U)^*$, clearly the duality pairing is given by
\begin{equation}
  \langle\sigma,\tau\rangle=\int_U\sigma\wedge\tau,\quad\sigma\in\om_c^{(p,q)}(U),\tau\in\dsheaf^{(p,q)}(U).
\end{equation}

From the previous definition, the space of $(p,q)$-currents is the dual space of the \emph{\bf Fréchet Space} of smooth $(n-p,n-p)$-forms where $(n-p,n-q)$ is called the bidimension of a current. This is the approach that I will use throughout this thesis where for every analytic set $A\subset X$ there is an association
\[
  \tau=\tau[A]\quad\text{with}\quad\langle\tau[A],\psi\rangle=\int_A\psi\quad\psi\in\om_C(X_{reg})^{(n-p,n-p)}
\]
These types of currents will be called an \emph{integration current} and I will soon show their properties which relate to the analytic structure of the underlying set à la Bishop.  
\subsection{Positive Currents and Closed Positive Currents}
\noindent Formally, integration currents are ``linear funcionals'', however geometrically are closer to volume forms of submanifolds, moreover, if $\tau_n$ denotes the invariant or "volume element" form on $\con$, that is $\tau_n=\omega^n/n!$, where
\begin{equation}
    \omega=\frac{i}{2}\sum_{j=1}^n dz_j\wedge d\overline{z}_j,
\end{equation}
the Kähler form on $\mathbb{C}^n$. The mapping $\tau_{I,J}\mapsto T_{I,J}:=\tau_{I,J}\,\tau_n$ canonically associates the coefficients of an integration current $\tau$ with generalized functions, here will be denoted as $T_{I,J}$. On the other hand, given a linear subspace $L^k$ of $\con$, it is possible to canonically associate a differential form $\omega(L^k)$ as follows; first, let $\co^k\hookrightarrow \co^n$, where $Z=(Z_k,Z_{n-k})\in\con$, and $Z_k\in\co^k$, then, one can use the description of $k$-dimensional complex vector space $L^k$, as

    L^k=\{(z_1,\dots,z_n)\in\con\,|\,z_{\alpha}=\sum_{\beta=1}^k a_{\alpha,\beta}z_\beta,\quad \alpha\in\{1,\dots, n\},\,\, a_{\alpha,\beta}\in\co\},
\]
 where the numbers $(a_{\alpha,\beta})_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{n,k}$ forms a matrix that extends to an element of the unitary group. Lastly define the following $(k,k)$ form
\[
    \tau(L^k):=\left(\frac{i}{2}\right)^k(-1)^{k(k-1)/2}\sum_{\Lambda,\overline{\Lambda}} \nu(\Lambda)\nu(\overline{\Lambda})dz_{\Lambda}\wedge d\overline{z}_{\overline{\Lambda}}.
\]
Where $\nu(\Lambda)=\|(a_{\lambda_1},\dots,a_{\lambda_k})\|$, also, $\Lambda$ and $\overline{\Lambda}$ are multiindices i.e. increasing sequences of elements in $\{1,\dots,n\}$ with length $k$. Thus it can be verified that $L\mapsto\omega(L^k)$ is an injective mapping from the Grassmannian $Gr(\con,k)$ of complex subspaces of complex dimension $k$ into the space of $(k,k)$ differential forms. It can be verified that $\omega(L^k)^{*}=\omega(L^{n-k})$ where $L^{n-k}$ is the orthogonal complement of $L^k$. Following Lelong's definition and notation for \emph{positive currents}
\begin{definition}\label{positive}
  A current $\tau$ of type $(k,k)$ with $0\leq k\leq n$ is positive if, for every subspace $L^{n-k}\subset\con$ of codimension $k$, the $(n,n)$-current defined as
  \[
    \sigma(\tau,L^{n-k})=\tau\wedge\omega(L^{n-k}),
  \]
  is a positive distribution (functional), this ensures that $\sigma(\tau,L^{n-k})$ is a positive measure.
\end{definition}
\noindent Here is a somewhat comprehensive account of propositions and definitions for positive currents that will be used throughout the rest of this thesis, they can be found in any geometric measure theory book, see \cite{federer}
\begin{theorem}
  The application $\tau\mapsto\sigma(\tau,L^{n-k})$ is injective and on the complex Grassmannian, also for any given point, $L_{0}\in Gr(\con,n-k)$, there exists a neighborhood of this point where it is possible to find points $L_s,\quad s\in\{1,\dots,{n\choose k}^2\}$, such that the coefficients of $\sigma_\tau=\sigma(\tau,L_0)$ are expressed as linear combinations of $\sigma_s=\sigma(\tau,L_s)$.
\end{theorem}
A system that satisfies the conditions in the previous theorem is called \emph{regular} for the similarities with \emph{regular coordinates}. As a consequence of the previous result ther is the following corollary
\begin{cor}
For a positive current $\tau$, the associated coefficients $\tau_{I,J}$ are complex measures, so $\tau$ extends to the space $\csheaf^0\om^{(k,k)}(U)$ of differential forms of type $(k,k)$ with continuous coefficients in $U$.
\end{cor}
Let $\dsheaf_{+}^{k}$ be the cone of positive currents of type $(k,k)$ (The set of positive currents is closed under multiplication by a positive constant). Let $\csheaf^0\dsheaf^{k}_{+}$ be the cone of positive currents that are representable by forms with continuous coefficients, positive currents of type $(k,k)$ are said to be of degree $k$ or of dimension $n-p=k$. If $\tau\in \dsheaf^{p}_{+}$ and $\phi\in\csheaf^0\dsheaf^{1}_+$, then $\tau_1=\tau\wedge\phi\in\dsheaf^{1}_{+}$. In particular the forms $\omega_n$, $\omega^{k}_n$ and $\omega(L^k)$ are positive, as is $i\dd\varphi$ if $\varphi$ is a plurisubharmonic function, for example, if $\tau\in\dsheaf^{n-k}_{+}$ is a positive current of degree $n-k$, then

    \end{equation}
    are positive measures, where the right-hand side of $\nu$ is the metric of the projective space at the point $\zeta$, normally denoted $\omega_{FS}$.

\subsubsection{Integration Currents}
On a complex analytic set \(A\subset U\subset\con\), we have the following theorem:

\begin{theorem}
The operator
\[
    \tau(\phi)=\int_A\phi
\]
defined by a simple extension of $\om_c^{(k,k)}(U')$ to the space $\om_c^{(k,k)}(U)$, exists and is unique and has three properties:
\begin{itemize}
    \item[a)] It is the unique extension of $\tau_0(\phi)$ that has zero norm on $\sig(A)$ (simple extension).
    \item[b)] It is closed.
    \item[c)] It is positive definite.
\end{itemize}
\end{theorem}

\noindent The proof is based on the "Stokes theorem for differential structures", see "Lelong P. - Intégration sur un ensemble analytique complexe", which basically says that if a closed current has a zero-order continuation in $U\setminus E$ where $E$ is a closed set, due to its extension from $U\setminus E$ to $U$, it is necessary and sufficient that there exists a family of $\csheaf^{1}(U)$ functions, say $\alpha_s(x)$ such that $\alpha_s(x)\geq0$, $\alpha_s(x)=1$ in a neighborhood $W_s$ of $E$ and whose support lies in a neighborhood $V_s$ of $E$, where $V_s\rightarrow E$ as $s\rightarrow 0$, also, more than that, we theed the following ``integration condition'' for the following limit
\[
    \lim_{s\rightarrow 0}\tau\wedge d\alpha_s=0.
\]
This condition is local and it is sufficient to construct such nuclei relative to compacts of $E$. In particular, if $E$ is the $\csheaf^1$ image of $\re^m$ of real dimension $m$, it is sufficient to prove
\[
    \lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{\|\tau\|^{r}_K}{r}=0,
\]
for every compact $K$, where we measure the norm as usual, but in
\[
 U_r=\{x\in K\,\,\vert\,d_H(x,E)<r\}
\]
The following result allows a simplification of the proof and its interesting by itself, a note before stating the proposition itself, the expression ``coordinate subspace'' of dimension $k$ refers to a subspace that given a given basis $\{e_1,\dots,e_n\}$ is written as linear combinations of $k$ elements of said basis, say \hbox{$L=\{\lambda_1e_{j_1}+\dots+\lambda_ke_{j_k}\}$}.
\begin{theorem}
Let $A$ be a pure $k$-dimensional analytic subset containing the origin and let $\mathcal{A}$ be the set of $L^{n-k}$ spaces that touch $A$ in such a way that the origin is not an isolated point of the intersection, then the following propositions hold for $\mathcal{A}$
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\mathcal{A}$ is identified with an analytic subset in the Grassmannian \(Gr(\con,n-k)\)

\item There exists an orthogonal coordinate system at the origin such that any $(n-k)$-dimensional coordinate subspaces at the origin does belong to $\mathcal{A}$

\item There exists a polycylinder relative to such a coordinate system say
\[
    \Delta^n=\{(z_1,\dots,z_n)\in\con\,|\,|z_j|<r_j,\,r_j\in\re^+\},
\]
such that the projections of $A\cap\Delta$ onto the $\co^{k}$ coordinate spaces are proper mappings.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
Taking Bishop's results and Chow's theorem in mind, this statement makes evident the existence of a ball $B=\{\|z\|<r\}$ such that
\[
        \sigma(r)\leq kr^{2k}
\]
which means that the analytic set \(\mathcal{A}\subset Gr(\con,n-k)\) is algebraic, since the growth order of the norm of the operator $\tau_0$ is polynomial as $r\to\infty$, moreover since the projections are propper, said coordinates are a set of regular cordinates for $A$ and the projections are a ramified covering map.

\subsubsection{Bounded Families of Analytic Sets}
In order to show the relationship bewtween Bishop's results and integration currents, consider the assosiation of an analytic suset with its integration current
\[
    A\mapsto \tau[A]\in\dsheaf^k_{+}=\{\tau\,|\,\text{ positive current}\},
\]
\noindent which associates to an analytic set $A$ of pure dimension $k$, a closed positive current $\tau[A]$, here, the I use the square braket notations to avoid confussion with the usual evaluation of a current. Therefore here its important to note that families of positive currents in a domain $U$ give rise to the same properties as families of positive measures. Here is the definition of the weak topology of currents

\begin{definition}
A sequence of currents \(\{\tau_n\}_{n\in\nat}\), converges weakly \(\tau_n\rightarrow\tau\) if and only if \(\tau_n(\phi)\) converges for every \(\phi\in\om_c^{(k,k)}(U)\)
\end{definition}
Here is a list of some useful properties of \emph{weak convergence} of integration currents, these can be found in a geometric measure theory book see \cite{federer}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item If $\{\tau_n\}_{n\in\nat}$ converges weakly, then $\tau=\lim\tau_n\in\dsheaf^k_{+}$. Moreover, the norms $\|\tau_n\|$ are \emph{bounded} on every compact set of $U$.

  \item If \(\fsheaf\) is a family of currents such that the norms $\|\tau\|$ are bounded for every compact set, then we will the family \(\fsheaf\) is called \emph{normal}, this implies that every sequence $\{\tau_n\}\subset\fsheaf$ has a weakly convergent subsequence.
  \item Consider the products
        \[
        \tau_n\wedge\omega(L^{n-k}_s)\quad\text{where }L^{n-k}_s\text{ is a regular system}.
        \]
        Let \(\fsheaf\) be a family of closed positive currents of dimension $k$ in $U$. Then for every compact $K\subset U$, the numbers \(\nu(\zeta,0)\), are bounded where
        \begin{equation}
        \nu(\zeta,r)=k!\pi^{-k}\sigma(\zeta,r)r^{-2k},
        \end{equation}
        Here $\sigma(\zeta,r)$ is the measure $\sigma$ previously defined on the ball $B_r(\zeta)=\{\|z-\zeta\|<r\}$ with $\zeta\in K$.
  \item If $\tau_n\rightarrow\tau$ weakly, and $z_m\rightarrow z$, then we have
        \begin{equation}
          \nu_{\tau}(z,0)\geq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\nu_{\tau_n}(z_n,0).
        \end{equation}
  \item In particular, $\nu(z,0)$ is an upper semicontinuous function.
\end{enumerate}
  
\begin{definition}
A family $\{A_i\,|\,i\in I\}$ of analytic sets of pure dimension $k$ is locally bounded in $U$ if their integration currents $\{\tau_i=\tau[A_i]\}$ form a locally bounded family in $U$.
\end{definition}

This is clearly a \emph{generalization} of the \emph{uniform boundeddness} of the volumes forms that I exposed before, specially the case of Kähler Manifolds.

\subsubsection{Properties of $\nu(z,0)$ for Analytic Sets}
The so called \emph{Lenong numbers} are the analytical equivalent to intersection numbers. For a divisor $D=\{f=0\}$, it is straight foward to calculate that if
\[
    f=P_q+P_{q+1}+\dots\text{where } P_j\text{ are homogeneous polynomial of degree }j\geq q,
\]
\noindent in a neighborhood around the origin, then the following a calculation similar to \ref{lelong-num1}, the Lelong number \(\nu\) can be shown to be
\[
    \nu(0,0)=q.
\]
On the other hand, an elementary proof gives the following; if $z\in A$ is an ordinary point for an analytic subset $A$, then $\nu(z,0)=1$. Thus it is straightfoward to prove that $\nu(z,0)\geq1$ for all $x\in A$. Moreover Thie proved that in fact Lelong are integers, i.e.  $\nu(z,0)\in\zah$.

\begin{prop}[Thie]\label{thie}
Let $A$ be a purely $k$-dimensional analytic set and let $z\in A$, then the Lelong number of its integration current can be calculated as
\[
    \nu(z,0)=\sum^{r}_{s=1}m_{s} m_{s}',
\]
\noindent where $s\in\{1,\dots,r\}$ is the index relative to the decomposition of the tangent cone at $z$ into irreducible components, say $T_1, \dots T_r$, where $m_s'$ is the degree of $T_s$ and $m_s$ is the degree of the volume form of $A$ over $T_s$ in a neighborhood of the origin.
\end{prop}

Now, having stablished the properties of Lelong Number's for analytic sets, its pertiant to define the Lelong subelevel set, as they will be used for singular foliations later. Let $X$ be an analytic complex (ringed) space, $c\in\re$ and $\tau$ a current on $X$, the \emph{Lelong sublevel set} of level $c$ of $\tau$ as
\begin{equation}
  E_c(\tau):=\{x\in X\,|\,\nu(x;\tau)\geq c\}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Some consequences}
If $\sigma(z,r)$ is the $k$-dimensional volume of $A$ in the ball $B_r(z)=\{\|z-x\|\leq r\}$ and $l(x,r)$ is the $(2k-1)$-dimensional volume of $A\cap\partial B_r(z)$, and if $\tau_{2k}$ and $\omega_{2k-1}$ are the volume forms of the open ball and its boundary respectively, then
\begin{align*}
\sigma(z,r)\geq\nu(z,0)\tau_{2k}r^{2k}\geq\tau_{2k}r^{2k}\\
l(z,r)\geq\nu(z,0)\omega_{2k-1}r^{2k-1}\geq\omega_{2k-1}r^{2k-1}
\end{align*}
\noindent In particular if $A$ is of dimension one, $l(z,r)\geq2\pi r$.
Thus, the class of positive currents that are currents arising from a complex analytic set $\tau=\tau[A]$, are distinguished by the following properties
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\tau\in\dsheaf^k_{+}$ 
\item $\tau$ is a closed operator
\item The number $\nu(z,0)$ in the suppot of $\tau$, $z\in \mathrm{supp}(\tau)$ has a lower bound $c\in\mathbb{R}^{+}$.
\item The number $\nu(z,0)$ with $z\in \mathrm{supp}(\tau)$ is an integer.
\end{enumerate}

Naturally the question arises: Are there positive currents that have the first three properties but do not come from analytic sets? The set of such currents will be denoted as $\Lambda$.

\begin{prop}
  If $\{\tau_n\}\subset\Lambda$ is a weakly convergent sequence of currents $\tau_n\rightarrow\tau$, then $\tau\in\Lambda$ (currents from non analytic sets are closed) and for the Hausdorff metric, there is the convergece of the compact sets
  \[
    \lim_{n\to\infty} \mathrm{supp}(\tau_n)=\mathrm{supp}(\tau)
\]
\end{prop}

For $\tau\in\Lambda$, the $(2k+1)$-dimensional Hausdorff measure of $\mathrm{supp}(\tau)$ is zero, i.e. the Hausdorff dimension of $\mathrm{supp}(\tau)$ is less than $2k+1$. It turns out that the set of $L^{n-k}$ spaces that intersect $\mathrm{supp}(\tau)$ at a point and such that their intersection contains a continuum, is of first category, i.e. it is a countable union of nowhere dense sets. Now in the context of Bishop's sequence theorem (theorem \ref{bishop sequence}), a basic rephrase of the stament in this context would be the following: If $\{A_n\}_{n\in\nat}$ is a sequence of pure dimension $k$ analytic sets in a domain $U\subset\con$ such that $\{\tau[A_n]\}$ form a bounded family of currents on every compact subset of \(U\), such that $A_n\rightarrow A$ is Hausdorff convergent, then $A$ is an analytic set.

Futhermore, it should be noted that there exists a coordinates system for $U$ such that the plane \hbox{$\{z_1=0,\dots,z_k=0\}\cap A$} is a disconnected set, which in terms of Bishop's proper mapping theorem (theorem \ref{bishop mapping}), and the previously mentioned sequence \(\{A_n\}\), means that, a point of $A$ has a ``hollow tubular neighborhood'' (more like just a hollow tube), of type $S\times T$ such that $(S\times T)\cap A=\emptyset$, this implies that $(S\times T)\cap A_n=\emptyset$ for all $n$ after a fixed $N\in\nat$. It follows that almost all sections of the $A_n$ by $S\times\{x\in T\}$ are of dimension zero (discrete points), this implies that the projection maps $p_n:A_n\rightarrow S$ are proper and have a degree $\lambda_n=|p_n^{-1}(\{x\})|$ which is bounded since the volumes are uniformly bounded. Therefore if $z_0\in(S\times T)\setminus A$, then one can define the following family of funcions $f_n(z)=p_n(g(z)-g(z_{i,n}))$ where the points $\{z_{i,n}\}$ are defined as
\[
\{z_{i,n}\}=p_n^{-1}[p(z)]\cap A_n,
\]
and where $g(z)$ is a holomorphic function on $S\times T$ with $g(z_0)\neq0$. The functions $f_n$ are holomorphic on $S\times T$ and converge uniformly to $f(z)$ (the corresponding one to $A$). Thus $A\subset\{z\,|\,f(z)=0\}$ and $f(z_0)\neq0$. Therefore $A$ is an analytic set.

\begin{theorem}
Locally bounded families $F$ of complex analytic sets are normal.
\end{theorem}

Indeed, every sequence $\{A_n\}\subset F$ has a subsequence $\{A_m\}$ such that $\tau_m=\tau(A_m)$ converges. Let $\tau=\lim \tau_m$, then
\[
    A_n=\mathrm{supp}(\tau[A_m])\rightarrow\mathrm{supp}(\tau)=A
\]
and as we know $A$ is an analytic set.

As showed previously these theorems are some of the consequences of the  
\begin{theorem}[Stoll's Theorem] If $B$ is an analytic set of $D$ and $A$ is a pure dimension $k$ analytic set in $D\setminus B$ and moreover $\|\tau(A)\|_{D\setminus B}\leq\infty$ then $\overline{A}$ is an analytic set in $D$.
\end{theorem}

\section{Introduction and motivation}
\noindent The notion of a ``singular Kählerian variety'' is an expression that is not well defined in the sense that there is not a definition with universal or near universal recognition in the field of complex algebraic geometry, that is, a definition with most if not all experts in the area agree on. Two proponents for what a definition of what a ``singular Kählerian variety'' the first was due to Grauert in 1962, where he defines it as a \emph{reduced ringed holomorphic space} $Y$ with a cover $\{U_\alpha\}_{\alpha\in\Lambda}$ such that there is a Hermitian metric $h$ and family of \emph{\textbf{strictly plurisubharmonic functions}} $\rho_\alpha:U_\alpha\to\re$ with $i\partial\overline{\partial}\rho_\alpha=h
$ locally outside of its singular set $S=\mathrm{sing}(Y)$ (see \cite{Grauert2}). However the definition that I will be using is due to Moishezon in 1974, the context for Moishezon's definition is that he proved that there is an equivalence between complex \emph{projective manifolds} and Kähler manifolds such that the number of \emph{algebraically independent} meromorphic functions equal the manifold's dimension. This condition is called \emph{Moishezon's condition}, and it turns out that for the definition he provided of \emph{singular Kählerian space} there is no such equivallence, however, more recent developments have shown that for Kähler spaces with isolated singularities, the equivalence holds true still.
\begin{definition}
  I will call an complex analytic space $X$ a \textbf{singualr Kähler} variety if it admits an open covering $\mathcal{U}=\{U_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\Lambda}$ and a family of \textbf{plurisubharmonic fuctions} $\phi_{\alpha}:U_{\alpha}\to\re$ such that in the intersections $U_{\alpha}\cap U_{\beta}$ the co-cycles $\phi_{\alpha}-\phi_{\beta}$ are \textbf{pluriharmonic}
\end{definition}
One of the reasons why I postulate that \emph{Moishezon's} definition for a Kähler varitey (that is potentially singular) is preferable for this work is that under favorable conditions, their structure is \emph{stable} in the sense of Kodaira-Spencer and this type jof stability has been shown to be very convenient for Gromov-Hausddorff convergence, which is a more general convergence theory that Haussdorff convergence for leaves in foliated spaces.

If the natural map $H^2(X_0,\re)\to H^2(X_0,\osheaf_0)$ is surjective (Variations of the Hodge structure, with Griffiths transversality), then every $X_t$ is also a normal compact Kähler variety for $t\in U\subset\Delta$ a small neighborhood of $0$.
  \end{theorem}

\section{Kähler currents and nef cohmology classes}
This part of my thesis based on the work of Jean Pierre Demailly and Mihail Paum both of which did substantian work on the cohmology classes of smooth positive definite closed $(1,1)$-forms, also know as the \emph{\bf Kähler cone}, they proved that the Kähler cone of a compact Kähler Manifold depends only on the \emph{intersection product} of the cohomoplogy ring, the \emph{Hodge structure} and the homology classes of \emph{analytic cycles}. For projective manifolds, this is a generalization of the \emph{Nakai-Moishezon} theorem which provides a necessary and sufficient criterion for a line bundle to be ample, and also links the so called \emph{numerically effective} or \emph{nef} for short, $(1,1)$-cohomology classes with its projective structure. This will be useful for the study of compact foliations in singular Kählerian varieties.

\begin{definition}[Numerically effective class]
  Let $X$ be compact Kähler manifold, a cohmology class $\{\alpha\}\in H^{(1,1)}(X,\re)$ is {\bf nef or numerically effective} is and only if there is a Kähler metric $\omega$ on $X$ such that
  \begin{equation}
    \int_Y \alpha^k\wedge\omega^{n-k}\geq 0\quad k\in\{1,\dots,p\}
  \end{equation}
  where $Y$ is any irreducible analytic set of dimension $p$. The set of all nef classes is denoted as $\mathcal{P}$, and is called the {\bf nef cone} for it is clearly closed under multiplication of positive reals.
\end{definition}
This definition however differs from the usual definition of \emph{numerically effective line bundle} in algebraic geometry, specifically in the metic sense a \emph{nef} line bundle $L$ on a complex Hermitian manifold $(M,\omega)$ is such that for every $\epsilon\in\re^+$ there is an hermitian $h_{\epsilon}$ on $L$, with the property that its curvature form satifies $\Theta_{h_{\epsilon}}(L)\geq-\epsilon\omega$. In the case of a class $\{\alpha\}\in H^{(1,1)}(X,\re)$, the corresponding inequality becomes $\alpha+i\partial\overline{\partial}\varphi_{\epsilon}\geq-\epsilon\omega$. Demailly and Paum proved the equivallence between both definitions of numerically effective in \cite{Demailly-Paum}, furthermore they proved that in a compact Kähler manifold the Kähler cone is one of the connected components of the numerically effective real $(1,1)$-cohomology classes. Clearly if I denote $\mathcal{K}$ to be the set of cohmology classes of smooth positive definite closed $(1,1)$-forms (the Kähler cone), meaning
\begin{equation}\label{big}
  \alpha\in\mathcal{K}\text{ if and only if }\int_{Y}\alpha^p>0\quad p=\dim(Y),
  \end{equation}
  where $Y$ is an \emph{irreducible analytic set}, also, if \(\alpha\) has the same integration property as in eaquation \ref{big}, then $\alpha$ will be called ``Big''. $\mathcal{K}$ is clearly a cone and by Bishop's theorems its clousure is an analytic variety, also, its clear that $\mathcal{K}\subset\mathcal{P}$. Demailly and Paum's result is an analogue of the famous \emph{Nakai-Moishezon criterion for ample line bundles} which classifies the \emph{Kähler cone} for algebraic projective manifolds as the set of all real $(1,1)$-cohomology classes for which their curvature is positive on analytic cycles (formal linear combinations of irreducible varieties). As its name suggest, the Kähler cone is the set of $(1,1)$-cohmology classes such with a representative that is a Kähler metric or the set of all Hermitian metrics that are cohomologous to a Kähler metric. As I have shown, the \emph{calibration} defined by a Kähler metrics means that they all are positive volume forms for every irreducible analytic sets, the result by Demailly and Paum these two notions are equivalent. 

  The dual view point (in the sense of integration currents) means that the \emph{\bf dual nef cone} $\mathcal{P}^*$ is the closed convex cone generated by the cohomology classes of currents of the form $[Y]\wedge\omega^{p-1}\in H^{(n-1,n-1)}(X,\re)$, where $Y$  runs over the collection of all irreducible analytic subsets of $X$ and $\omega$ over the set of Kähler classes of Hermitian metrics of $X$. The point of view of integration currents will be very useful here, as I have shown from the work of Lelong himself, the stability of limits analytic sets can be studied with limits of their integration currents, for the work presented here a special type of currents will be necessary.

\subsection{Kähler currents}
\noindent For the case of \emph{sigular Kähler varieties} using Kähler metrics is not possible due to the non-smoothness due to singular set, therefore there is less restrictive notion of a \emph{\bf Kähler Current}
\begin{definition}
Let $X$ be a compact complex ringed space, a Kähler current on $X$ is a {\bf closed and positive} current $\tau$ in the sense of \ref{closed} and \ref{positive} which satifies $\tau\geq\epsilon\omega$ for some $\epsilon\in\re^{+}$ and $\omega$ smooth positive hermitian form in $X_{reg}$ 
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}
    Let $X$ be a compact complex manifold, and $\tau$ a positive $(1,1)$-current, then
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item The class $\{\tau\}$ is {\bf nef} if and only if its restriction $\{\tau\}|_Z$ is {\bf nef} for every irreducible component $Z$ of the Lelong sublevel sets $E_c(\tau)$.
      \item The cohomology class of a Kähler current $\{\tau\}$ is the class of a {\bf Kähler} form if and only if its restriction $\{\tau\}|_Z$ is a {\bf Kähler class} for every irreducible component $Z$ of the Lelong sublevel sets $E_c(\tau)$.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

Now, for compact complex manifolds its known a way to determine whether or not
its possible to define a \emph{Kahler current} on them, this is known as the
\emph{\bf Fujiki class} $\csheaf$ as it was defined by \emph{Akira Fukiji}. A
manifold is of Fujiki class $\csheaf$ if and only if its \emph{dominated} by a
compact Kähler manifold, meaning its the image of a meromorphic function of a
Kähler manifold.
\begin{theorem}
  A compact complex manifold carries a Kähler current if and only is dominated by a Kähler manifold, more succinctly, the manifold is in the Fujiki class $\csheaf$ 
  \end{theorem}
In some sense, Demailly and Paum generalized this result for singular complex varieties, the type of varieties that will turn out to be Kähler in the sense of Moisheson's.
When $X$ is a non singular compact complex manifold, it is convenient to cosider the \emph{pseudoeffective} cone $\mathcal{E}\subset H^{(1,1)}(X,\re)$, the set of cohomology classes of positive $(1,1)$-currents. By weak compactness of bounded sets, $\mathcal{E}$ is a convex cone. When $X$ is Kähler, then $\mathcal{K}\subset\mathrm{int}(\mathcal{E})$, moreover, a Kähler current $\tau$ has a class $\{\tau\}$ in $\mathrm{int}(\mathcal{E})$ and conversely, any class (\emph{big} class) $\{\alpha\}\in\mathrm{int}(\mathcal{E})$ can be reresented by a Kähler current (see \cite{Demailly-Paum}), in fact, every nef cohomology class with \emph{strictly positive} self-intersection of maximum degree, contains a Kähler current. The following lemma will bridge these results of Kähler currents and Moishezon's Kählerian varieties:
\begin{lemma}
  Let $X$ be a compact complex manifold equiped with a Kähler metric $\omega$ and let $Y\subset X$ be an analytic subset of $X$, then there exist $\psi$ and $\{\psi_{\epsilon}\}_{\epsilon\in[0,1]}$ a family of quasiplurisubharmonic potentials satisfying the following:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item[i)] $\psi$ is smooth on $X\setminus Y$ and satifies
          \[
          \partial\overline{\partial}\psi\geq -A\omega\quad A\in\re^{+}
          \]
          
    \item[ii)] $\psi$ has \emph{logarithmic poles} along $Y$, this means that in a neighborhood of $Y$
          \[
            \psi(z)\approx\ln\left(\sum_k|g_k(z)|\right)+O(z)
          \]
          where $\{g_k(z)\}$ is a local system of generators of the ideal sheaf $\mathcal{I}(Y)$ of $Y$ in $\osheaf(X)$.
    \item[iii)] $\psi_{\epsilon}\to\psi$ where $\epsilon\to 0$ such that all $\psi_{\epsilon}\in\csheaf^{\infty}$ are smooth and have an \emph{uniform Hessian estimate} $i\partial\overline{\partial}\psi_{\epsilon}\geq A\omega$.

    \item[iv)] The family of hermitian metrics $\{\omega_{\epsilon}\}$ with
          \[
            \omega_{\epsilon}:=\omega+\frac{1}{2A}\partial\overline{\partial}\psi_{\epsilon}\geq\frac{1}{2}\omega.
          \]
          Have a \textbf{uniform lower bound} for their volumes elements regular points $y\in Y$. Meaning that for each regular point $y\in Y$ and any neighborhood of $y$, if $V_{\epsilon}=\{z\in X\,|\,\psi(z)<\ln(\epsilon)\}$ is a neigborhood of $Y$ such that  there is a $\delta(U)\in\re^{+}$ independent of $\epsilon$ with
          \[
            \int_{U\cap V_{\epsilon}}\omega_{\epsilon}^{n}\geq\delta(U)>0
          \]
    \item[v)] For every non-negative integer $p\in\nat$, the family of positive currents $\omega^p_{\epsilon}$ is bounded in mass. Moreover $Y$ contains an irreducible component $Y'$ of codimension $p$ there is an \emph{uniform lower bound} in any neighborhood $U$ of a recular point $y\in Y'$
          \[
          \int_{U\cap V_{\epsilon}}\omega^p_{\epsilon}\wedge\omega^{n-p}\geq\delta_p(U)>0.
          \]
  \end{enumerate} 
\end{lemma}
Note that point $v$ implies that any weak limit $\Theta$ of $\omega^p_{\epsilon}$ as $\epsilon\to 0$ satisfies $\Theta\geq\delta'[Y']$ for some $\delta'\in\re^{+}$. Where $[Y']$ represents the class of intregration current of $Y'$.
\begin{proof}
  Take a finite covering of $X$ by open balls $\{B_j\}^N_{j=1}$ such that $\mathcal{I}(Y\cap\overline{B_j})=(g_{j_k})^{m_j}_{k=1}$ and let $\{\theta_j\}^n_{j=1}$ a partition of unity asociated to said covering with
  \[
    X=\bigcup^{N}_{j=1}B_j\quad\text{and}\quad \sum_{j=1}^N \theta_j^2=1.
  \]
  With this, define the following functions
  \begin{align*}
    \psi(z)&=\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\sum^N_{j=1}\theta^2_j(z)\left(\sum^{m_j}_{k=1}|g_{j_k}(z)|^2\right)\right)\\
    \psi_{\epsilon}(z)&=\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(e^{2\psi(z)}+\epsilon^2\right)=\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\sum^N_{j=1}\theta^2_j(z)\left(\sum^{m_j}_{k=1}|g_{j_k}(z)|^2\right)+\epsilon^2\right).
  \end{align*}
  Now consider the following family of $(1,0)$-forms with support in $B_j$ for each $j\in\{1,\dots,N\}$; $\{\gamma_{j_k}=\theta_j\partial g_{j_k}+2g_{j_k}\partial\theta_j\}^{m_j}_{k=1}$ applying the operator $\overline\partial$ on the fucntion $\psi_{\epsilon}$, it easy to see by a simple calculation that
  \[
    \overline{\partial}\psi_{\epsilon}=
    \frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{j_k}\theta_jg_{j_k}\overline{\gamma_{j_k}}\right)\frac{1}{e^{2\psi}+\epsilon^2}
  \]
  Therefore
  \begin{align*}\label{psi-e}
    i\partial\overline{\partial}\psi_{\epsilon}=&\frac{i}{2}\left(\frac{\sum_{j_k}(\gamma_{j_k}\wedge\overline{\gamma_{j_k}})}{e^{2\psi}+\epsilon^2}-\frac{(\sum_{j_k}\theta_j\overline{g_{j_k}}\gamma_{j_k})\wedge(\sum_{j_k}\theta_jg_{j_k}\overline{\gamma_{j_k}})}{(e^{2\psi}+\epsilon^2)^2}\right)\\
    &+i\frac{\sum_{j_k}|g_{j_k}|^2(\theta_j\partial\overline{\partial}\theta_j-\partial\theta_j\wedge\overline{\partial}\theta_j)}
    {e^{2\psi}+\epsilon^2}.
  \end{align*}
  Now by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality
  \[
  \left| \sum_{j_k}\theta_j\overline{g_{j_k}}\gamma_{j_k} \right|^2
    \leq\left(\sum_{j_k}\theta^2_j|g_{j_k}|^2\right)\left(\sum_{j_k}|\gamma_{j_k}|^2\right)
    =e^{2\psi}\left(\sum_{j_k}|\gamma_{j_k}|^2\right)
  \]
  therefore
  \begin{align*}\label{lowbound-e}
    \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\sum_{j_k}|\gamma_{j_k}|^2}{e^{2\psi}+\epsilon^2}
    -\frac{\left|\sum_{j_k}\theta_j\overline{g_{j_k}}\gamma_{j_k} \right|^2}{(e^{2\psi}+\epsilon^2)^2}\right)&\geq
    \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\sum_{j_k}|\gamma_{j_k}|^2}{e^{2\psi}+\epsilon^2}
                                                                                                               -\frac{e^{2\psi}\left(\sum_{j_k}|\gamma_{j_k}|^2\right)}{(e^{2\psi}+\epsilon^2)^2}\right)\\
    &=\frac{\epsilon^2}{2(e^{2\psi}+\epsilon^2)^2}\left(\sum_{j_k}|\gamma_{j_k}|^2\right)
  \end{align*}
  Now, the sum of the terms $\theta_j\partial\overline{\partial}\theta_j-\partial\theta_j\wedge\overline{\partial}\theta_j$ is \textbf{uniformly bounded} by a \emph{fixed} hermitian form $-A\omega$ with $A\in\re^{+}$ by Wirtinger's inequality, so this two inequalities prove that $i\partial\overline{\partial}\psi_{\epsilon}\geq -A\omega$ forall $\epsilon\in[0,1]$. Moreover, for every pair of indices $(\alpha,\beta)$ there are $c<C$ positive reals such that
  \[
    \frac{1}{c}\leq\frac{\sum^{m_\alpha}_{k=1}|g_{\alpha_k}|^2}{\sum^{m_\beta}_{k=1}|g_{\beta_k}|^2}\leq C\quad \text{on the sets}\quad\overline{B_{\alpha}}\cap \overline{B_{\beta}}.
  \]
  Since $g_{\alpha_k}$ can be written as a linear combinations of $g_{\beta_k}$ by Cartan's theorem A, \ref{CartanAB}. It follows by compactness that  all $|g_{j_k}|^2$ are  uniformly bounded by $e^{2\psi}+\epsilon^2$. This proves that $\psi$ and $\psi_\epsilon$ are plurisubharmonic.
  Now, let
  \[
    \omega_{\epsilon}:=\omega + \frac{1}{2A}i\partial\overline{\partial}\psi_{\epsilon}
  \]
  Then clearly by construction $\omega_{\epsilon}$ satisfies $\omega_{\epsilon}\geq 1/2\omega$, hence $\omega_{\epsilon}$ is Kähler and  its eigenvalues with respect to $\omega$ are at least $1/2$.
  Now at a regular point and neighborhood, $x_0\in U\cap Y$, the previously defined 1-forms are expresded as $\gamma_{j_k}=\theta_j\partial g_{j_k}$, therefore if $Y$ has codimension $p$, then $(\gamma_{j_k})$ has rank at least $p$ in $U$. By the regular coordinates theorem, there are local coordinates for $U$, $(z_1,\dots,z_n)$ such that $Y\cap U=V(z_1,\dots,z_p)$, let $S$ be the sub-bundle of $TX(U)$ spanned by the tangent vectors defined by the local coordinates $(z_1,\dots,z_p)$, meaning
  \[
    S=\Bigl\langle\dfrac{\partial}{\partial z_1},\dots,\dfrac{\partial}{\partial z_p}\Bigr\rangle\quad\text{with stalk denoted as}\quad S_z.
  \]
  The min-max theorem states that to in a finite dimensional complex vector space $V$ and $\omega$ herminitan and form with spectrum in descendinge order $\lambda_1\geq\dots\geq\lambda_n$, the following holds
  \[
    \lambda_k=\max_{\dim(W)=k}\min_{\|\xi\|=1}\omega(\xi,\xi)=\min_{\dim(W)=n-k+1}\max_{\|\xi\|=1}\omega(\xi,\xi),
  \]
  this applied to $\omega_{\epsilon}$, and $S_z$ means that its $p$-largest eigenvalue is bounde below by $c\epsilon^2/(e^{2\psi}+\epsilon^2)^2$. Now, let
  \[
    \tau(\xi)=\sum_{j_k}\theta_j\overline{g_{j_k}}\gamma_{j_k}(\xi)
  \]
  Similarly, applying the min-max theorem to the restriction of $i\partial\overline{\partial}\psi_{\epsilon}$ to $S\cap\ker(\tau)$, then by \ref{psi-e} and \ref{lowbound-e} means that the $p-1$ largest eigenvalues of $\omega_{\epsilon}$ are bounded from below by $c/(e^{2\psi}+\epsilon^2)$, the remaining $n-p$ eigenvalues are bounded by $1/2$, therefore
  \[
    \omega^n_{\epsilon}\geq \frac{c\epsilon}{(e^{2\psi}+\epsilon^2)^{p+1}}\omega^n\quad\text{and}\quad\omega^p_{\epsilon}\geq \frac{c\epsilon}{(e^{2\psi}+\epsilon^2)^{p+1}}\left(i\sum_{1\leq l\leq p}\gamma_{j_l}\wedge\overline{\gamma_{j_l}} \right)^p
  \]
  where $(\gamma_{j_l})$ is a sutable $p$-tuple extracted from $(\gamma_{j_k})$ such that $\cap\ker(\gamma_{j_l})$ is a smooth complex but not necessarily holomorphic sub-bundle of codimension $p$ of $TX$, this bundle has to be $TY$. To finish the proof, note that for a \emph{tubular neighborhood} of $Y$, which can be written as in point \emph{iv)} $V_{\epsilon}$, now for a regular neighborhood $U$ of $x_0\in Y$, the following hold up to constants
  \[
    e^{2\psi}+\epsilon^2\thicksim |z_1|^2+|z_2|^2+\dots+|z_p|^2+\epsilon^2  
  \]
  and
  \[
    i\sum_{1\leq l\leq p}\gamma_{j_l}\wedge\overline{\gamma_{j_l}}\geq c\,i\,\partial\overline{\partial}\ln(|z_1|^2+\dots|z_p|^2)-i\,O(\epsilon)\,\partial\overline{\partial}\ln(\|z\|^2)
  \]
  therefore
  \[
    \omega^p_{\epsilon}\wedge\omega^{n-p}\geq i\,c\left((\partial\overline{\partial}\ln(|z_1|^2+\dots|z_p|^2+\epsilon))^p\wedge(\partial\overline{\partial}\ln(|z_{p+1}|^2+\dots|z_n|^2))^{n-p}\right)
  \]
  Also, by previous inequalities its easy to see that $\omega^n_{\epsilon}\geq 2^{-(n-k)}\omega^p_{\epsilon}\wedge\omega^{n-p}$, therefore any lower bound of $\omega_{\epsilon}^p\wedge\omega^{n-p}$ is a lower bound of $\omega^n_{\epsilon}$. Note that $i\,\left((\partial\overline{\partial}\ln(|z_1|^2+\dots|z_p|^2+\epsilon)\right)^p$ is a pertubation of the \emph{Fubini-Study} $p$-volume form, therefore is the volume form of an affine Zariski open set rescalted by $\epsilon$, hence taking $\epsilon\to 0$, the pullback converges weakly to the integration current of
  \[
    Z=\{z_1=\dots=z_p=0\}
  \]
  moreover, the volume contained in any compact tubular cylinder
  \[
    \{\|(z_1,\dots,z_p)\|\leq c\,\epsilon\}\times K\subset\co^{p}\times\co^{n-p}
  \]
  depends only on $c\in\re^{+}$ and $K$. The fact that $\omega_{\epsilon}^p$ is \emph{uniformly} bounded in mass near $Y$ (points \emph{iv) and v)}) follow from the fact that
  \[
    \int_{U\cap V_{\epsilon}}\omega_{\epsilon}^p\wedge\omega^{n-p}=\int_{U\cap V_{\epsilon}}\omega^n,
  \]
  which mean that $\omega$ and $\omega_{\epsilon}$ are in the same Kähler class. Now, taking the \emph{weak limit}
  \[
    \Theta(\xi)=\lim_{\epsilon}\omega^p_{\epsilon}(\xi).
  \]
  The previous inequalities, implies that $\Theta$ carries non-zero  mass on every $p$-codimensional component of $Y$ for instance the regular component. By Skoda's extension theorems and the support theorem for currents $\mathds{1}_{Y_{reg}}\Theta$ is a closed positive current such that
  \[
    \mathds{1}_{Y_{reg}}\Theta=\lambda\,\tau[Y']
  \]
  \end{proof}
